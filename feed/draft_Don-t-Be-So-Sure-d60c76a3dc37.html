Don’t Be So Sure

Remember that everyone — including you — is a dumbass.

Don’t Be So Sure

We don’t see things as they are. We see things as we are. — Anais Nin
Between believing a thing and thinking we know is a short step and easily taken.— Mark Twain

Looking around at public discourse nowadays, two things stand out: 1) the tsunami of nonsense that has found widespread acceptance, from decisive voter fraud in the 2020 election, to QAnon, to the dismissal of public health guidance on the COVID pandemic; and 2) the intolerance with which all views, nonsensical or not, are bandied about. Collectively these are an existential threat to democracy, and individually, it seems that the lamer we get, the angrier we get. Could there be a connection?

I submit that the common thread between epidemic ignorance and ideological intransigence is unwarranted confidence. We have a plague of certainty. Certainty lubricates the spread of bad ideas in a community, and transmutes opinions into dogma; intolerance is just certainty with thorns.

Everyone immediately concurs with that assessment…as long as it’s about Them, the fools out there who refuse to see the light. The trick is to realize that this isn’t just true of other people. We’re all experts at spotting and denouncing unwarranted confidence in others, but that position assumes that some of us are right and some of us are wrong, and that politics is for sorting out one from the other. But what if we’re all wrong? What if none of us has the first clue about reality as it is?

I propose that we could throw some cold water on the overheated political climate by looking at this certainty that has us beating each other over the head.

Ignorance as a Way of Life

If there’s one thing we’re certain about, it’s certainty itself: we may make the odd mistake, but by and large our senses and our minds give us a pretty complete, pretty reliable picture of the world as it is. After all, we don’t spend our days metaphorically stumbling blindly into doors or falling downstairs, so how far off base could we be? But avoiding the worst collisions with reality (and surviving to propagate our genes) is a pretty low bar. 

We have an unshakeable faith that our senses deliver an accurate picture of the world to our brains, and that the limits of what we can know are as clear-cut as the boundary between light and shadow. Both of those are not just untrue, but so ludicrously untrue that it makes us look like a pack of monkeys running around thinking they’re at the top of the world.

Think for a moment about our limitations comprehending size, scale, and complexity. Does a nanometer (1 billionth of a meter) mean anything to you? Imagine taking a yardstick, taking a tenth of it, then taking a tenth of that, then taking a tenth of that, then taking a tenth of that, then taking a tenth of that, then taking a tenth of that, then taking a tenth of that, then taking a tenth of that, then taking a tenth of that. Either you gave up before the end of that sentence or your head imploded. Yet a single water molecule is a fraction of that size. And you’d have to do that whole process another nine times to get down to the scale of a quark. 

Now go in the opposite direction: multiply the size of the yardstick by ten, nine times. You’ve barely gotten to the size of the moon’s orbit around earth. Go another eight times and you’re at about the distance between the sun and its nearest neighbor star. And you’re still only one ten-thousandth the size of the Milky Way. It takes nine more iterations to get to the size of the observable universe.

Does that all fit in your head? It doesn’t fit in mine.

One reason people resist the theory of evolution is their utter inability to conceive of the vast time scale over which a microbe evolves into, say, us. They just can’t imagine there’s enough time. If you think of the age of the earth (4.5 billion years) as a day, the entire history of the human species would fit in 3 seconds. A human lifespan would be a micro-blink: about 1/500th of a second.

It’s not that some people can fit such an eternity into their heads and the doubters can’t. Nobody can; it’s just that some can accept that such scales exist whether we can conceive of them properly or not.

In our minds, we catalog things and give them names, and that catalog is how we deal with the world. We see boundaries and identities, and we interact with things one at a time. But what is invisible to us in any meaningful sense is the web of relationships that make up the natural world. The word ecology was coined just a few decades ago to give a name to the complexity with which natural entities interact. If you have a system of three entities, there are only three possible relationships between pairs. A system of five has 120 pairings to contend with, and after that, the number gets just as unfathomable as the size or age of the universe.

As with the universe, and with systems, so with quantum physics. Here we are, on our little island of clarity, straining our facilities — which were provided solely to help us survive — and dismally failing to comprehend anything much smaller, larger, more enduring, distant or complex than us, our puny, puny selves.

The Universe is not only queerer than we suppose, but queerer than we can suppose.” (J.S. Haldane)

But taking it back to where we live. Think about where you are — literally, where your self resides: in your skull. Your mind is confined to a capsule of meat within a bone shell atop another pile of meat and bones. Your brain is utterly isolated: it’s only way of knowing anything about the world is via electrical impulses fired by sense organs on the outside, carried on neural pathways in the body and then laboriously interpreted by the brain. Our eyes, for example, have a low-resolution array of sensors on the retina, only a small part of which (the fovea) is dense enough to be of much use, the remainder of the visual field being filled in by mental reconstruction. There’s literally a blind spot where the nerve fibers pass out of the eye, and we are blind to it because of this filling-in process. Most of the eye is insensible to color, and the bits outside the fovea provide mainly information about motion. Yet we “see” a complete picture of the world — or so our brain would have us believe.

What accounts for this gulf between the amount/quality of the data we sense and the richness of the world we perceive? Our brains literally fill in the blanks, using incoming data as cues that trigger patterns that live in our memory. The brain merges the data and the memories, synthesizing a complete, detailed picture that it then blesses with confidence, clarity and certitude. Perception is less a matter of reception than it is of conception.

Optical illusions are a vivid (as it were) reminder of how strong this filling-in process is. One of my favorite experiments involved showing subjects a screen full of text — or so it appeared. In reality, the screen was almost all gobbledygook: only the small fraction that the subject viewed at a given time (an eye tracker was used to tell where that was) actually showed clear text. In the time it took for the viewer’s eye to shift from one place to another and settle in place, the screen could be re-drawn. The subjects were unshakably convinced that they were viewing a perfectly ordinary screen of text.

Getting a true picture of the world outside via neurophysiology is like getting the play-by-play of the Super Bowl over a telegraph: some information gets through, to be sure; recipients will unfailingly marshall all their resources of verbal interpretation, prior experience and imaginative visualization to bring it to life. They will surely construct a far more compelling picture than what they’re getting, but, unlike brains in skulls, no one getting the telegraphic version would mistake it for the whole story.

…and that’s just what we can see, feel, etc. directly. Almost everything else we know about the world has to come to us from other brains through language with untold passings through words and other minds on the way to us. Yes, our vision and hearing extend outward through communications technologies, but how much do we learn over the wire without accompanying words?

Now turn your attention inward, and think about who you are on the inside: the richness, intensity and variety of your interior life. How much of that is available to others? Does anybody else have the first clue what’s really going on in there? To feel that gap directly, recall the rush of joy and elation that wells up whenever we come to feel, however fleetingly, that someone else “really gets me”. This marks as well as anything how much we’re locked inside our own skins. 

But turn that around. Do you imagine that you understand other people any better than they understand you? If you’re a mystery to others, then every one of us lives in a dark wood full of inscrutable shadow-figures. What claim does any one of us have to know more than the wispiest fraction of the human life around us?

…and what do we know about ourselves, anyway? Think of the half-million mental health professionals in the US whose job is to help people figure themselves out. And yet we walk around sublimely convinced that we have a clue.

Recall a moment in your life when you were thunderstruck by revelation. A time when you opened your eyes just the right way and awe rushed over you as you contemplated something in the vastness of nature, or a moment when another person revealed themselves, truly, and you understood something profound about the human condition. Everyone has those moments (I hope), and any one of them will do. That blinding flash of revelation is nothing more or less than your default blinkers slipping momentarily.

Do you think that the thing you suddenly understood sprang into being the moment you had your revelation? Don’t you think there is an infinite number and variety of other revelations to which you are walking around utterly oblivious? Those magic moments not only throw a spotlight on some hitherto dark portion of the world, it should tell us how much darkness still lies outside.

In the end, our position isn’t that different from the hookworm. We compare our exquisite senses with its near-zero levels of sensation and cognition, and it’s easy to imagine that we are looking down from some privileged position. But what if we could look up? What makes us think that we’re at the limit of that scale? Surely we can’t look up and apprehend creatures who are as far above us as we are above the hookworm, but we can get a glimpse of other such worlds in the visual acuity of the hawk; the frequency sensitivity of dogs; the unfathomable spectrum of color available to the bluebottle butterfly with its 15 types of color receptors; the mental capabilities of a savant like John von Neumann. But really, the scale vanishes into darkness pretty quickly.

You get the idea. Compared to what we don’t know, what we do know is not just the tip of the iceberg, more like a snow cone on the tip of the iceberg. We wander an infinite landscape of possible knowledge, in total darkness except for a few dapples of light, but we somehow remain unaware of the dark and think we’re in broad daylight.

Why this ludicrous gulf between what we think we know and what we actually deserve to believe? What’s the advantage of being so blissfully ignorant? It helps to ask a second question: what is the purpose of understanding what’s going on? We evolved the capacities we have for a reason: survival. And surviving requires two things: recognizing threats and opportunities in the environment; and acting on those understandings. These can become competing concerns. It doesn’t pay to act on unreliable information, but it also doesn’t pay to worry too much about imperfect information if it delays acting. And in a life-or-death situation, the important thing is not just to act, but to act quickly. With conviction. Accuracy and completeness are secondary concerns.

In other words, our senses and our cognition didn’t evolve to give us a clear and complete picture of reality. They evolved to give us a good enough picture of reality, as defined and strictly limited by the needs of survival. But at the same time, we evolved a sense of certainty, a faith in that accuracy, so that we could act to live another day.

Now take that ocean of ignorance and delusion in which we swim, and apply it to the modern political debate, if you can call it that. I don’t know about you but I hear nothing but the voices of people who are trying to prove that they’re right that they have the way. It reminds me of a giant game of King of the Hill played on a mountain of shit.

It gets worse. Suppose we decided to reform, to call out our inner philosopher, set reflexive certainty aside, put our beliefs through the wringer, and reserve our confidence for beliefs that actually hold water. Here’s the shocking thing: making that assessment is actually, literally impossible. To begin with, much of our thinking — by far the bulk of it — is actually unconscious/intuitive, and thus not even available for inspection. But assessing even the thoughts that you can get to is also a matter of thinking, which thinking must also be assessed, and so forth, infinitely down the rabbit hole. 

No, a truly valid decision on what to believe is just beyond us.

A personal note: I belong to a community that has a privileged, if embarrassing, view into the workings of a brain on certainty: software developers. Every programmer has this experience: your gleaming image of a perfectly functioning program is colliding with the reality that it just isn’t working. The refrain here is something along the lines of “how can this possibly not work?” There ensues an extended period of banging the image and the reality together, as stress, frustration, perplexity and profanity proliferate. Finally, after titanic struggles with the demons of the dark, the world shifts, the brain unlocks, all is revealed, and a new thought moves in: “How could this EVER have worked, AT ALL?!?”

Confidence is not and cannot be a reliable indicator of correctness.

If your purpose is to get a complete and accurate picture of the world, your brain is not your friend. It has its own agenda, shaped by our own evolutionary history, which only seems to be leading us to the truth — and notice the double-edged sword in that statement. Your brain presents itself — convincingly — as a reliable witness, and it covers the ideas it presents you with in a sugary coating of conviction and certainty. We need to get a better handle on both.

Out of the Bind

Before I make you completely hopeless about this aspect of the human condition, I have some good news. It turns out that, a few centuries ago, humanity as a whole developed an ingenious hack to deal with the problem of unwarranted certainty, one that both improves the accuracy of our conclusions and limits excess confidence: Science.

On a nuts and bolts level, science provides a practical bullshit detector for ideas: probing for logical inconsistencies, shaking them down for clashes between the theory and the facts of the matter, testing predictions to see if they pan out. These are amazingly powerful tools that account for the staggering progress of science and technology over the last few centuries.

But the real lesson of Science is more subtle. It directly addresses the “paradox of knowledge”, the need to proceed confidently as if you were correct, while remaining cognizant of the possibility of being wrong.

When confronting a paradox, the natural urge is to make a choice and commit to one pole or the other. In the case of the Knowledge Paradox, it’s easy to see the damage done by people who seize on either pole: those who will not question their beliefs under any circumstances, and those who won’t believe any expert who ever made a misstatement or a mistake, as well as the postmodernist for whom no idea is worth investing in.

But, as is often the case with paradox, there is a third possibility hidden in plain sight: to embrace both poles; to plunge in with full confidence, while staying open to later course correction. To keep an open mind in the midst of fervent belief.

Doesn’t that sound like Science? The genius of Science is to take all knowledge as provisional while working fervently to make it as solid as possible. We believe the theories that currently prevail — believe in them enough to build new theories atop them — but we are rigorously open to revising them in the light of new data and/or arguments.

You may be a skeptic about Science as it is practiced. I will join you in that skepticism: Science is practiced by imperfect human beings, imperfectly, sometimes falling onto one pole of the paradox or the other. The scientific consensus often changes its mind, seemingly arbitrarily (looking at you, purveyors of health and nutrition advice), and there is an arrogance that often comes in on the heels of such a spectacularly successful strategy, that believes that science is the only viable path to knowledge.

Still. 

Science never seems to get close to objective truth, or even to something we can really depend on. But Science is not after perfect knowledge, valid for all time. Science has given up that pipe dream, and admitted that it could be wrong. And therein lies its strength and its genius. Because not expecting to decide anything finally is exactly what allows science to get closer to the truth than any other method of extracting knowledge from the world.

This is a radical tradeoff that multitudes still struggle with. 

The Power of Certainty

The quest for certainty is, to put it mildly, an enduring aspect of the human experience. You can see it in the appeal of a God who has all the answers. 

You can see it in the dismissal of experts who are sometimes wrong, even if their advice is the best available. 

You see it in the attraction to authoritarians and ideologues who dangle comprehensive worldviews that Make Sense Of It All Despite What They Want You To Believe.

You see it in the hunt for abstractions and principals which explain everything.

You can see it in the power the converted feel in their certainty, the joy and relief on the faces of people plunging into “ism”s like libertarianism, racism, socialism, capitalism.

You can see it in the eyes of someone whose certainty is threatened, who is faced with relinquishing their certainty and going back to a state of ambivalence and a process of (again!) sorting things out.

But you see it most in the drive to finish investigating and questioning, and come to a conclusion which will make investigation and questioning a thing of the past.

Certainty and clarity are very serious matters, seriously pursued. The drive to acquire them, to cloak our deliberations in them, to rest our anxious souls in them, is universal, relentless, and largely unconscious. And they are so ubiquitous as to be invisible — until you start looking at humanity through that particular lens.

Life Without Certainty

The quest for clarity and certainty even at the price of correctness is so strong that it raises a question: What would it be like to let it go? What could we get in return?

Without such a powerful drive to reach a destination, we would be free to poke around the landscape of ideas, wandering onto a side road here, lingering for a while there, getting to know the territory in all its idiosyncracies.

Without the need to defend and justify our conclusions, we would be open to engaging openly with others and their ideas, exploring the landscape together in a process that’s less a wrestling match and more of a dance.

Without confusing our ideas with ourselves, and their viability with our self-worth, we could be more serious about questioning, clarifying, exploring and strengthening them.

Beliefs don’t need to be like coins we collect and guard jealously. They could be more like playthings. With a more jaunty attitude we might hold more than one incompatible idea at once, playing them off against one other, or no idea at all, browsing the possibilities. We could endure the silliness of taking an idea from one domain and using as a metaphor in another.

Without mistaking our beliefs for objective reality, we could get past the need to impose them on other people. We might even be able to glean some compassion, or even respect, for others in their own flawed quest for understanding.

…but none of this means we take ideas less seriously; they still are the gears in the machinery of democracy. It’s paradoxical but true: ideas matter, but they still need to be handled deftly. The mantra I like is “strong ideas, lightly held.”

This ceaseless quest for certainty reminds me of Al Pacino’s Satan character in The Devil’s Advocate, trying to get his son Keanu Reeves to stop feeling guilty:

You know, I’ll tell you, boy. Guilt…it’s like a bag of fucking bricks. All you got to do…is set it down.

Epistemic Humility

The whole problem with the world is that fools and fanatics are always so certain of themselves, and wiser people so full of doubts. — Bertrand Russell

In the interest of loosening the grip that the drive to certitude holds on humanity, I would like to propose that we start looking at it as a vice, one closely related to hubris: the vice of assuming that we are in any position to figure out The Way Things Are beyond the immediate need to stop reflecting and take action. The vice of believing that we have a uniquely privileged view of the world, such that everyone else must be misguided or delusional. This vice has always been with us, but isn’t it plain to see how catastrophic it has become?

But I prefer to look on the positive side. In contrast to this vice-without-a-name, I propose a new virtue. Because I suck at marketing, I call it epistemic humility.

What is a virtue? A virtue is a moral value, and a moral value exists to steer us in one direction when immediate circumstances want to send us another way.

A virtue is a hook for modeling. When we see someone of limited means making a sacrifice to help another, that would just be puzzling without understanding the virtue of selflessness. I’m not religious at all, but the Quaker faith does have a compelling tenet: teaching by example. Quakers believe that there’s no preaching virtue, no imposing it on others. It can only be modeled. You can’t make anyone else stop misbehaving; you can only show how to behave.

A virtue is something we teach our children and/or our students as an ideal to aspire to, and value that becomes part of our culture, if only aspirationally. We don’t expect all of our fellows to be kind, generous, chaste, etc. But we do admire those who are, and we at least aspire to be more like them.

Finally, a virtue sets up a standard of behavior, a default. Once a virtue takes root, the most bald-faced unvirtuous behavior becomes conspicuous and potentially shaming. Public standards of behavior cause people to feel obliged to keep up appearances, at least in public. It might become a matter of shame to go out into the world raving about voter fraud, kind of like taking a shit in the street. Wouldn’t it be nice if the worst actors on the public stage got a wee bit less shameless?

Epistemic humility is kind of like AA: it surrenders before the impossibility of true understanding given the hopeless position we are in, and to our credulity in the face of the influences from within and without: on the outside, the social pressures and information silos whose mission is to enlist our belief for any purpose other than real justification; on the inside, our limited senses and attention, our hardwired biases, our need to feel certainty.

I propose epistemic humility as a virtue — a public virtue — because humanity is fucked if we don’t get our brains around some basic ideas: that looking at the first three things that come up on a Google search constitutes “research”; that we are at the mercy of the impressions formed by a politically-motivated mono-diet; that the reality in which each of us lives is increasingly constructed by the attention economy; that certainty is untethered from correctness.

Life Under Uncertainty

Doubt is not a pleasant condition, but certainty is absurd — Voltaire

But how is this virtue to be pursued, by the pioneers who pursue it? I don’t have a code of ethics for this new world, but I do have a few modest suggestions.

Most general, and most diffuse, is eternal vigilance against signs of closing off in oneself. Adam Grant, in his new book Think Again, highlights three modes of thinking that reflect a closed mind: the preacher, the prosecutor and the politician. The preacher is working to make converts; the prosecutor’s job is to find flaws in the opposition and make a case by whatever means necessary; and the politician is trying to convince by schmoozing and charming, to make it easy to believe. It will be far easier to spot others operating in these modes than to self-identify, but it’s worth the practice, and that framework might eventually get recognized within.

I strongly recommend Grant’s book for anyone with an interest in lightening their grip on their beliefs and their thinking. He brings in clinical and social data and far longer case studies in what works and doesn’t work to change minds (including one’s own) than there is room for here. Instead, let me suggest Ten Commandments of Epistemic Humility.

Keep your Identity Small

Beware the sense of humor failure. A wise man once told me that the hallmark of a cult is losing their sense of humor, and in my subsequent experience it has turned out to be a surprisingly powerful test. When you lose the ability to find any humor in yourself or your ideas, it’s time to stop and take stock.

Don’t expect your intelligence to save you. Intelligence works just as well for rationalizing and defending as it does for exploring and solving. In my experience, one of the outstanding aspects of smart people is that they can talk themselves into anything.

Understand the opposition’s position better than your own. This is called steel manning. It not only makes your own ideas stronger, it’s uniquely disarming when you can articulate the opposition’s position better than they can.

What would it take to get you to change your mind? Asking this question, either of yourself or others, disrupts the confirmatory chain of thought and opens one’s conviction to pointed inspection.

Say things out loud. Talking to other people isn’t just a way of persuading those cretins on the other side. It’s a way of holding your own ideas up to scrutiny and seeing if you really know what you’re talking about. It’s amazing what happens when those moldy ideas from your mental attic get brought out and exposed to oxygen and sunlight.

Get others to say things out loud. I’m talking about listening without trying to persuade, and in so doing draw others into saying it out loud. Just by the act of listening, the limitations of their views may become obvious even to themselves. The opposite of defensiveness is openness, and you can get the latter just by inviting it.

Preface declarative statements with “I believe” or “It seems to me”. Even with no other changes to your conversational style, just those simple phrases are a devout expression of humility, and they introduce a context that turns any argument over facts into a discussion of why this thing is believed (which may or may not be a matter of fact). 

Learn the difference between having strong opinions and being opinionated. Paradox is one of my favorite things, and this is one of my favorite paradoxes: there is no contradiction between holding a strong belief and being open to changing it. 

Remember the purpose of confidence. The reason I’ve hammered on certainty as an epiphenomenon is that it does have a life of its own, almost independent of actual correctness. It was critical for survival when we evolved, and it’s critical for getting off the dime and taking action even now. But it’s always worth having the question lurking in the back of your mind: “Do I really need to be so sure?”

Cultivate comfort with not knowing. After spending enough time skeptically observing people and their thought processes, it’s hard not to be struck by their urge to state the case. I don’t know if it’s a personal inclination or learned skill, this state of not knowing, but I do know that a final determination, or just a working hypothesis — no matter how provisional — is definitely optional equipment going forward, in the absence of good data.

What I’m trying to describe is a world of the mind in which the craving to know finds its proper place (deciding and getting the hell on with things, and holding out for what must be believed, rather than what could be believed), but otherwise gets out the way and leaves us in a playground of the mind in which ideas are toys to be tried out, played with, banged together and taken as far as they can go but no further — not used as weapons, or as wedges to drive people, and countries, apart. It’s a positive vision for addressing the grim direction that our world seems to be headed, both online and off. I don’t know if this idea will make any difference, but I have a pretty scary idea of what will happen if we don’t manage to change direction somehow.

All views are bad views

Yes we have no choice but to proceed as if what we can make out of the world is accurate. What alternative do we have, really? And it’s part of the magic of consciousness that we can sometimes feel them in our bones as the right thing. But we should never forget that it is just a feeling, one which may or may not be accurate. And will never know about that any more than anything else.
