The Asshole Problem

Donald Trump, it is commonly observed, has a number of character flaws: compulsively untruthful, delusional, narcissistic, graceless, impulsive, willfully ignorant, thin-skinned, incapable of expressing regret or handling criticism, entitled, self-pitying, disdainful, shameless, disloyal, selfish, insensitive, lazy, bullying, vindictive, crude, grandiose, hypocritical, vulgar, empty of empathy or compassion, manipulative, mocking of the dead and the disabled, sexually predatory, humorless, cruel and vain.

We have a good word for people like that. We call them “assholes”, and we put a high priority on avoiding them.

This is what I’m calling The Asshole Problem: the fact that the chief executive of the United States government and default leader of the free world is an asshole. You would think that the fact that such a situation is a problem, and that something should be done about it, would be self-evident. But the evidence suggests otherwise: something like one in three American adults don’t know, don’t care, or actually like the fact that their man is an asshole.

…and that’s the second version of The Asshole Problem: the struggle to have it make a difference politically, so that said asshole can be replaced with a bona fide human being. Surprisingly, this is a problem, and such a situation is pretty much unprecedented. Normally, for voters to get a whiff of even one of Trump’s many personal shortcomings would detonate any electoral aspirations. And yet here we are, facing a significant chance of him being RE-elected. How can this be?

Let me start with a temperate thought for my fellow Trump haters: don’t be too quick to judge. Is it so obvious that Trump’s public should automatically abandon him just because he’s an asshole? Who among us would give up on someone we looked to for change we desperately wanted, even if (s)he was an asshole? Thinking back a few years, I have to ask myself: what would I have tolerated in Barack Obama, if his heart and his shoe leather were in the right place? If he could get a constitutional amendment passed securing a woman’s right to choose, would finding out that he was a pussy-grabber drive me into the arms of, say, Mitt Romney? If he slam-dunked universal health care in America, wouldn’t I find a way to forgive him for mocking a disabled person? If he got nuclear and/or fusion power on its feet and put the US on track to go carbon-negative in twenty years, would I get the screaming fantods over a demented Twitter feed? I confess: I doubt it — not just by looking deep within my own soul, but because I still think fondly of Obama despite grave reservations about a number of his policies. Is a little assholery that much more to bear?

My point is that objections to Donald Trump on grounds of character, however forceful, are a matter of degree, not kind. It reminds me of George Bernard Shaw’s (alleged) conversation with a female society swell at some tony soirée. He asked her if she would sleep with him for a million pounds. “Why Mr. Shaw, that would certainly be an offer difficult to resist!” she replied. Shaw: “Well then, would you sleep with me for 10 pounds?” “CERTAINLY NOT! What do you think I am?!?” Shaw: “Madam, we have already established what you are; we have only to settle on the price.”

We all have our price, is what I’m saying.

The two sides of this coin aren’t quite the same, though. I could see myself carrying on with Obama despite him being an asshole, but I wouldn’t do so because of it. And it’s hard not to suspect that Trump’s being an asshole is what his base likes about him. The way he “tells it like it is.” The way he “sticks it to the libs.” The way he swings a bludgeon at the status quo. Just randomly sampling his Tweets and his rallies is convincing enough evidence that being an asshole is good for him.

But whether it’s ignorance, or forgiveness, or relish that’s operating here, trying to convince his followers that he’s an asshole is a fool’s errand. So why is the anti-Trump punditocracy and commentariat still trying? If criticism at that level has no effect, it’s not just a colossal waste of time. To the extent that critical discourse meant to change minds focuses on matters where no minds will be changed, to that extent it is neutered. Rendered powerless.

And this is the very core of the Asshole Problem: this focus on Trump as an asshole is not only misguided as a focus of attention, but is self-defeating in practice and, in the end, a strategic disaster that is helping Trump drive American democracy off the rails. In short, “Trump Derangement Syndrome” is real — just not in the way that his minions mean.

Why is it “misguided”? First, there’s the opportunity cost of pursuing that line of argument. In the attention economy, attracting viewers/readers/listeners and keeping their attention is the coin of the realm, and those who are already seething with contempt for Trump are the easiest marks. How many times has Seth Meyers replayed that footage of Trump walking away from the press pool and wandering aimlessly around the White House lawn, or ascending to Air Force One with toilet paper stuck to his shoe? How many damning, demented but patently cherry-picked clips from his rallies, or clips of him stumbling over some words? Classic preaching to the choir: would I change my mind about someone I was already supporting by seeing any of this? Would it have any effect on me other than turning me off and shutting me down?

The anti-Trump media are foregrounding content whose goal is to make the already-convinced feel warm and fuzzy in their contempt for Trump, but it’s not going to change minds on the other side. So, beyond virtue signaling and audience stroking, what’s the point?

But why is the Trump-as-asshole theme a “strategic disaster”? Because the cognitive effort to ignore his character spills over into more substantive matters. Working to give it a pass instills a more broad-based defensiveness about all matters Trump. Having once bound up their identity with his, and having it hardened by what appears to them be gratuitous criticism, they have been recruited to defend not just his personality but his policies and his governance in general.

To appreciate this phenomenon, it might be helpful to think in terms of the illusionist’s use of misdirection. The performer can get up to all kinds of mischief behind the scenes or in their pocket or up their sleeve as long as the audience’s attention is distracted elsewhere. Developing skills in misdirection is close to the heart of the illusionist’s art.

Donald J. Trump gets much of his power with a, let’s face it, ingenious use of misdirection in the public sphere. By getting respectable people to home in on his status as an asshole, he indirectly inoculates his followers against any real scrutiny of the things that matter: his criminality, his egregious policies and his hapless governance. His opponents get sidetracked, and his cheering squad adopts a defensive mode that gets applied to his conduct in general.

I have seen the same point made about Trump’s lying. He lies so blatantly, about things that are so readily fact-checked, that lying must have some other purpose than convincing the listener of anything. And it does. Swallowing Trump’s lies is both a loyalty test for supporters (how much can they take?) and desensitization training against objections to other matters. As with the lies, so with being an asshole.

Let me invite the reader to a thought experiment. Envision a smooth-talking sociopath, someone who had polished their image to a sheen. If such an impeccable figure climbed the electoral tower and swanned into office, then proposed Trump’s exact policies and had the same hapless record, how would this person fare? Without having softened the ground with an offensive history, I imagine such an Elmer Gantry figure facing far more resistance than the asshole.

How does Trump get his base on board with tax cuts that leave them behind? How does he keep the support of workers and low-income people through attempts to gut Obamacare? How does his abject failure to “bring the jobs back” or provide for coal miners or resist getting rolled by China still play in the heartland? Democrats have anguished for years over that question. I believe the answer, in large part, is: Because he’s an asshole. Not despite. Because.

I wish I could proffer a brilliant solution to the Asshole Problem. I really do think that American democracy has not confronted such a phenomenon before, and consequently we are very poor about dealing with it in and of itself. But what to do about it?

Time for another thought experiment. Imagine the leader above, who was scrubbed clean of all Trump’s personal shortcomings, but pursued exactly the same policies, undermined the same institutions and assembled an equally hapless administrative cohort. What would happen if the press, media and individuals treated Trump with rigorous respect, pretended that he was a reasonable human being, and focused exclusively on his policies and actions?

What would happen if the media took Trump seriously, as though he could be held accountable in the same way a respectable leader would? What if we took him seriously and pretended that he isn’t an asshole? I have a fantasy press conference in my head, in which questions like the following are posed:

“Mr. President, you recently tweeted out your advice to Florida voters to take advantage of vote-by-mail, saying that it is safe and secure. Can you tell us the difference between Florida’s system and those you still think are corrupt and subject to fraud?”

“Mr. President, infectious-disease experts warn of a second massive wave of Coronavirus infections in the Fall. What steps are you taking to deal with such a second wave?”

“You made reviving the coal industry a centerpiece of your election. How are the coal miners doing?”

“You became famous some years ago for questioning the birth of Barack Obama. Have you changed your mind about that, and if so, have you ever apologized to him for questioning his legitimacy?”

“You have made your feelings very clear about mail-in voting in this year’s election. Can you outline for us the steps you are taking to ensure safe access to in-person voting, so that voters don’t have to face long lines or find themselves de-registered?”

“If Ruth Bader Ginsberg leaves the Supreme Court this year, will you be nominating a replacement, given that Mitch McConnell ‘saved’ a seat for you to appoint?”

Doesn’t that feel good? Doesn’t that feel like progress? I think it does, and I think that the lesson, such as it is, is that there is no substitute for focusing on the substance of a presidency, regardless how tempting a target is its progenitor — no matter the moral hazard presented by the party in question.
