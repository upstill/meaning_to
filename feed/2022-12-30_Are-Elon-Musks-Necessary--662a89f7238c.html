Are Elon Musks Necessary?

The Asshole Theory of History

Are Elon Musks Necessary?

What a source of raging ambivalence is Elon Musk. This is the guy who made electric cars cool, thereby taking a bigger bite out of climate change than pretty much any individual, ever. This is the guy who reawakened space travel and will, it seems, bring high-speed Internet to the remotest places on Earth. BUT…he is also the bull in the china shop at Twitter, dismissing key employees by the thousands, impulsively suspending accounts for personal reasons and generally thrashing around, all knees and elbows, in a situation that calls for a cool head in charge. A history-making figure who has more than a whiff of the sociopath about him.

It’s tempting to believe that those poles can’t be separated, the genius and the sociopath. Indeed, it’s part of the Great Man archetype, the manly man who rides roughshod over any and all obstacles. (This is distinct from a normal leader who can inspire others to follow their lead without being an asshole about it.) Certainly the movers and shakers of Silicon Valley agree, whether publicly or privately, per the New York Times:

They admire him for ruling Twitter with an iron fist and making the kinds of moves that tech executives have resisted for fear of alienating workers — cutting jobs, stripping away perks, punishing internal dissenters, resisting diversity and inclusion efforts, and forcing employees back to the office.

Of course Elon Musk is only the current exemplar. History is strewn with such figures, from Steve Jobs to Robert Moses to Ronald Reagan to Donald Trump to Winston Churchill, and on and on. So you’ve got to wonder: if you want earth-moving change, does it take an asshole? In other words, are Elon Musks necessary?

This is a serious question for a place as seemingly stuck as the present-day US, struggling to effectively address its many generational challenges when its last collective achievement was the moon landing half a century ago. It recalls Richard Nixon’s image of America as a pitiful, helpless giant in the face of Communism, only this time the enemy is us.

So will it take an asshole to fix us? Do we have to choose between continuing to languish or putting an asshole in charge and giving him or her free rein?

Assholes Don’t Mind Breaking a Few Eggs

One of the reasons it’s tempting to say Yes is that there is an aspect of destruction in pretty much any progress. As they say, you can’t make an omelet without breaking some eggs. For example, real progress on carbon emissions is essentially incompatible with continuing to burn coal, which just is seriously bad news for the coal miners.

Anyone who makes tough choices will bear a heavy burden of responsibility, their only comfort the fairly abstract idea that the net outcome is positive, all things considered. It takes a certain kind of person to cope with those impacts…but does it take an asshole? It does seem at least possible to compartmentalize without sacrificing your humanity, and the person who taught me that was Barack Obama.

Here was a man who bore the weight of the Presidency, whose every moment in office had real impact on ordinary lives across the globe. You would think that a person in such a position would devote every waking moment to optimizing that impact. And yet, here was a guy who could, every evening, put down that burden to focus on his family. I don’t know how anyone’s ego can avoid being crushed by such responsibilities, but it doesn’t seem to require a sociopath — though they do have a natural advantage.

But there is another class of situations where assholes have a distinct advantage, situations condensed nicely in the Trolley Problem.

Of Trolleys and Assholes

The Trolley Problem is a thought experiment in ethics. A trolley is rolling down the tracks, its brakes failed. A group of five people are on the tracks, about to be killed. But wait! There is a siding to which the trolley can be diverted, and you have the switch in hand to do that. The problem is that there is one person standing on the siding, and that person will die if you pull the switch. What do you do?

Human response to the Trolley Problem has been studied in experiments without number; results are mixed and hotly debated by both psychologists and ethicists, but one thing is clear: people are powerfully troubled by pulling that switch. They have a deep aversion to taking any action that might cause harm even when the costs of inaction are greater.

How reluctant are we to take action that might cause harm? This question goes directly to the heart of the Asshole Problem if you assume that the key trait of an asshole is willingness to plunge in anyway, consequences be damned. So let’s look a little closer, with some more experimental results, graciously provided by Daniel Kahneman in his classic Thinking, Fast and Slow. Consider your response to the following two scenarios:

1: You have been exposed to a disease which if contracted leads to a quick and painless death within a week. The probability that you have the disease is 1/1,000. There is a vaccine that is effective only before any symptoms appear. How much would you would be willing to pay for the vaccine?

2: Volunteers are needed for research on the above disease. All that is required is that you expose yourself to a 1/1,000 chance of contracting the disease. (No vaccine exists in this scenario.) How much would they need to pay you to volunteer for this program?

The two scenarios probe the difference between undertaking a positive action vs. simply mitigating an existing issue. In theory, you should be willing to pay the same amount of money to relieve an existing risk as you would ask to take on the same risk voluntarily. But people expect a lot more money to take on a risk than they would pay to eliminate it — and I do mean a lot: Richard Thaler’s experiments found it was typically fifty times as much.

What’s going on here? The main thing seems to be the potential for regret. If Scenario 2 turns out badly, the one who took on that risk is far more regretful than the actor in Scenario 1 who simply failed to take action. This is why it’s such a struggle to get people to wear masks and get vaccinated: they’d much rather deal with the consequences of inaction than take positive steps toward prevention.

Thaler’s colleague Cass Sunstein calls this the Precautionary Principle, which rules our personal decision-making but also plays out at grand scale in all kinds of governance. It ensures that the risks of taking action loom large — massively, critically large — overwhelming any prospective positive outcome. Sunstein lists all sorts of innovations that exist only because someone overcame precaution: “airplanes, air conditioning, antibiotics, automobiles, chlorine, the measles vaccine, open-heart surgery, radio, refrigeration, smallpox vaccine, and X-rays.” Those someones had to stick their necks out, swimming upstream against precaution, to make those things happen. And who but the asshole is better suited to do so? “Damn the torpedoes, full steam ahead!” is the cry of one who is invulnerable to regret.

The Precautionary Principle dictates the conservatism of bureaucracies of all kinds. Remember “Nobody ever got fired for buying IBM”? Or “It’s easier to get forgiveness than permission”? There’s a thunderous truth behind those sayings. When you go for a permit for a home project, or take out a loan, that requires someone to sign on — give their permission — for a positive action. It puts them in a scary and vulnerable position full of potential for regret, compared to letting it slide in retrospect. No wonder bureaucracies often feel like they’re hard-wired to thwart invention and innovation.

Progress is Risky

The Trolley Problem and the Precautionary Principle are compounded by the fact that novelty and innovation come bundled with uncertainty and risk. It’s not obvious that bold initiatives will work out; in fact, they may leave us worse off than before.

There were many reasons, good and bad, to object to Obamacare, but the stark reality is that it was presented as a way to solve the problem of 45 million Americans without healthcare, when far more people already had healthcare that by and large they were happy with. Those numbers were laden with risk to the status quo for an uncertain gain. Of course there was resistance! The potential losses loomed too large over the potential gains, and the resulting package was significantly compromised.

Assholes have no such problems, since they can narrow their vision to potential gains, dismissing potential losses and dismal odds of success.

So yes, we resist taking positive action, and we also, reasonably, worry about uncertainty and risk; the Elon Musks of the world are tailor-made to power through both. But there is another reason such figures are so appealing just now, and that reason is most clear when you look at the case of Donald Trump.

Frustration and the Appeal of the Asshole

Donald Trump offered his supporters many things, but high up on the list was his function as a wrecking ball. His deepest support came from people who felt passed over, ignored by the powers that be on matters ranging from immigration to globalization to cultural change. They truly felt the need for an asshole, to the point of missing that he was too wrapped up in his own self-glorification to actually do anything more than channel that frustration and own the libs. That that was enough is a powerful statement of the appeal of the asshole.

But the MAGA movement was just the canary in the coal mine for a stark fact: America doesn’t feel like it can do anything anymore, and such a vacuum seems designed to be filled by an asshole. But it gets worse. Why is America stuck? Because, in the absence of forceful leadership, positive action requires public support, and that in turn is hard to come by without a certain level of trust in public institutions: trust that they will have your interests at heart, trust that they will keep their promises, and trust that they can deliver the goods.

If that seems abstract or obscure, consider the response in this country to the COVID pandemic. In The Atlantic magazine, Derek Thompson compares that recent experience with the invention of vaccines and the total eradication of smallpox from the world. It’s a pretty stark contrast, and he makes a convincing case that the crux of the matter in the case of COVID was failure of trust and the spirit of cooperation, rejecting the counsel of scientific institutions and government, and raging at the blandest public health measures. Common sense measures with plenty of precedent that might have been taken up easily became incendiary.

Nothing is more inimical to the success of positive community action than lack of public trust. And when people don’t trust their institutions because they have failed to deliver, it sets up a doom loop: lack of trust begets institutional failure, which begets frustration, deeper distrust and suspicion of institutions, further undermining trust. And on it goes, spiraling down. Does that sound familiar?

Will America ever be able to voluntarily achieve anything as a nation again? Not if it’s stuck in such a doom loop. Go far enough down that spiral, and the allure of the strongman, the authoritarian, the asshole blossoms. Unless America can recover a semblance of public trust and faith in institutions, the writing is on the wall: some version of Elon Musk, or Donald Trump, or Ron DeSantis, or Jai Bolsonaro, will become inevitable.

Necessary? Or Inevitable?

Are Elon Musks necessary? At the very least, they have intrinsic advantages over normal human beings. On the one side you have leaders like Barack Obama who seem hampered by their very reasonableness and by their inclination to build a big tent. On the other, we have the assholes, who don’t have their egos crushed by the consequences of their actions, who don’t let the fear of regret inhibit them from taking action, who can pull the switch on the trolley. Are those advantages decisive? Should we give up hope that normal leaders might overcome their limitations, and instead just buckle up for a turbulent ride?

The real question might be: are Elon Musks inevitable? Here the prospects are pretty grim. As prosperity makes societies more risk-averse (because they have more to lose), and as public trust in institutions and one another frays, the wheels of progress get more and more bogged down. And as the missed opportunities pile up, as crying needs go unanswered, as elites and institutions seem increasingly unresponsive, frustration builds…and builds…until it seems like putting an asshole in charge is the only choice.

I wish there were an easy, obvious way to prevent such a dire outcome, but the best we can hope for is to twig to the slippery slope and be alert for the many temptations offered by the asshole. If things keep going the way they seem, we’ll be setting ourselves up more and more for the assholes of the future. But if we can recognize the temptation and where it’s coming from, maybe we can resist just a bit more.
