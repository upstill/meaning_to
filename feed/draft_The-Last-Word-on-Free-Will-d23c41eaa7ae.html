The Last Word on Free Will

Do we have free will, are we the captains of our own fate? Is everything pre-ordained by brain chemistry? Are we just leaves blowing in the winds of circumstance?

For most people, I reckon, the question is both trivial and persistently unsettling. Trivial, because the matter seems decided by a little introspection: I feel like I decide what I do, and I like feeling in control. Besides, what difference does it make whether I’m the original author of my own intentions or not? Don’t spoil my complacency by making me think about it too much. 

Fair enough.

But it’s unsettling because it leads directly to questions of personal responsibility. When someone behaves badly, either socially or criminally, then if that person was at the mercy of the universe, how fair is it to punish them? That’s just (re)action directed at the wrong target.

That tug of sympathy for the miscreant is one of the bigger differences between Right and Left, at least historically (modern-day political hysterics have different fish to fry), with one side focussing on a Biblical model of punishment for sins, while the other tends more to look for underlying causes (i.e., upbringing in poverty, childhood neglect and abuse, various personal psychiatric disorders, etc.).

While I’ve always been sympathetic to the addressing-underlying-causes side of things, I really didn’t want to give up on the idea of holding people accountable; wouldn’t accepting some flavor of determinism make that impossible to avoid, and set us on a slippery slope to anarchy?

Something I read a long time ago brought a lot of clarity to my thinking about this, and now I offer it forth to you. In a meeting in the California governor’s office with Herman Kahn, the question of free will came up and he remarked

What kind of people do you get? If you act as if there’s free will and people are responsible for their actions, you get one kind of person. If you act as if people and everything is determined and they’re not responsible for their acts, you get a very different kind of personality…And I don’t care what the real world has to do with this, because even if it’s a deterministic world you get a preferable kind of people by treating them as though they have free will.

From one perspective, it’s quite unsettling to think that everything we say and do is predetermined. If we as individuals are not in control, then who is?

Determinists ask, in the moment after you say or do anything, how could it have been otherwise? Perhaps you could have decided to do something different beforehand, but how could that have happened otherwise? And so on from there. Thus we are led to an infinite regress that to determinists sounds like a trump card, and to others (not naming names here) seems like the kind of cute philosophical argument that doesn’t mean anything.

For most of us, the matter is settled by simple experience, at least at the personal levelBut at the societal level, it becomes quite a sticking point. When someone else commits a crime (say), how harshly we judge them depends largely on how responsible we think they are for their actions

I think it’s fair to say that the question of free will lies just below the surface of arguments about crime and punishment. If a person has little control over their decisions, and thus their actions, there’s little to be said for a punitive attitude, vengeance aside. But if free will pertains, if the criminal was responsible for their actions, then punishment, while it may or not be a moral imperative, seems more like a reasonable option.

This argument shows no prospect of being resolved as long as the contenders bring their unspoken assumptions about personal responsibility. Even if they did lay their cards on the table, the argument would still rage on.

But I’ve always felt that there was a tension here, something looming unspoken over the whole debate. And I think I’ve finally put my finger on it:

Holding a person responsible for their actions is a reasonable course whether free will exists or not.

It’s obviously true when people do have free will. But if they don’t — if their actions are wholly determined by history and context — then an important part of that context is what will happen to them after they misbehave. In other words, determinism is no excuse for removing one of the factors that determines people’s choices.

In the immortal words of Herman Kahn, 

Whether free will exists or not, you get a better class of people if you assume that it does.

So, underlying a belief in punitive justice (again, setting aside motivations of vengeance) is an assumption that people are responsible for their actions. And behind much of the sentiment in favor of a more lenient approach is that they’re not. But if the question of free will is effectively moot, then where’s the beef?

Please, I don’t mean to imply that this realization would transform the debate. But it would help if everybody was barking up the right tree.
