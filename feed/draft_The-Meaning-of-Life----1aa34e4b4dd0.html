The Meaning of Life?!?

We are meaning-making machines, and the sooner we realize it the better.

A Life of Meaning

Is anyone else as annoyed as I am by the question “What is the meaning of life?”

I’ve long been fascinated by the idea that our brains are in the business of reporting objective reality. The truth is that those brains are much more adept at making us believe they do that than they are at actually delivering veridicality. For decades, we’ve been awash in behavioral economics, data about cognitive bias and countless other streams bent on separating us from that impression, yet we remain unbowed. We don’t really know what to do with this information.

There are areas in thinking about thinking where meaning is found a legitimate and useful place (Buddhist psychology), but for the most part, what something is is all tangled up with what it means. And learning to tease the two apart turns out to be a surprisingly useful and productive endeavor.

Meaning-making in psychology

I recently had a very trying (to say the least) experience with meaning-making which permanently cemented its importance in my mind. In the course of working within a group, I committed some egregious errors. (Pardon the vagueness here; the details aren’t important, and the privacy of the parties involved is.) Nothing as bad as sexual assault or anything else sexual, and absolutely without malice or any ill intention, but still: bad enough to make me permanently ashamed to have failed so badly. And the reaction was cataclysmic. Two members of the group permanently wrote me off, and through a social process that I wasn’t privy to, I became a pariah and a scapegoat for the group at large.

Now, what to do with this? In some sense, what I did caused that reaction. Does that mean I have to take full responsibility for it? It seems that there’s a real disproportionality between the nature and intention of my actions and the ultimate impact, but how do I draw a boundary between the two? It seems like the other parties involved must have some role in the outcome but what could that be, when the spectre of “blaming the victim” is so real? The fact that I meant no harm doesn’t count for much. How can I not blame myself for everything that happened? How do I avoid the conclusion that I’m a horrible person? I want to take responsibility for what I did, but how to square that with the seeming disproportionality of the ultimate outcome?

The idea of meaning-making rescued me from this quandary. I did certain things, and those things had an effect. The actions and their direct effects were what is. But in the event, some of the others in the group chose to derive the absolute worst, most uncharitable meaning from those blunders, starting with the initial response and ending with ostracizing me. Other meanings could have been derived from the facts of the matter, and I can’t be responsible for which meanings prevailed.

Analyzing the meaning-making in situations like this doesn’t eliminate wiggle-room or judgment calls. All it does is establish a continuum, from inevitable, to reasonable, to unreasonable, to hostile and beyond. But it does provide the tools to think about the proportionality of response without either letting the perpetrator off the hook or making them responsible for everything that might result from a given action.
