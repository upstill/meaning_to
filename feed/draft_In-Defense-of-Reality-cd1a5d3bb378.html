In Defense of Reality

I hope that the above title conveys a little of how ludicrous our current predicament is. Like the battering that our norms and institutions have taken in the few short months of the second Trump presidency, it shouldn’t have to be said that paying due attention to the way things are, and acting in accord with that information, is just a better way to succeed than making things up as you go along. But here we are. It needs to be said.

…and yet, it’s surprisingly difficult to make the case that people who at least try to sail toward truth are better off than those who wander about steered this way and that by whatever pops into their heads or whom they last talked to. So I’ve attempted a little review of why realism is a good idea — generally speaking. 

A Note of Temperance

The Way Things Are isn’t always the most important thing. Sometimes, behaving as if something is true makes it so. (self-fulfilling prophecy) This is especially true in the social realm, in which the seemingly impossible comes to life simply because the right people become convinced it’s true. The classic case is a bank run: a financial institution may be perfectly sound, but if a critical mass of account holders decide that it’s not, their departure can trigger a confidence collapse that really does destroy the institution. Human affairs are rife with situations that are literally governed by beliefs: civil order depends crucially on the belief that civil life is orderly; functional interpersonal relationships thrive in an atmosphere of trust, and vice versa; even something as simple as showing up for a meeting depends on a mutual belief in the timeliness of others.

Conversely, great movements of people can be marshaled for astonishing achievements if word gets around that doing something about it is a) good and b) possible.

In cases like these, reality itself is built on a tissue of agreement and common perception. 

But we’re not here to talk about that.

So let me review what fealty to the truth actually brings to the party. But first, a philosophical sidebar: 

Following is a list of advantages that accrue when your perception matches reality.

Making change without detailed information makes you a bull in the china shop, heedlessly blundering around. Even supposing that border control and restricting immigration are indeed what you want doesn’t mean that the first, most obvious and appealing policy is the best way to achieve it. Some of us believe that there was no border “crisis” that couldn’t be addressed pragmatically and compassionately with Congressional mandates for clear yeah-or-nay criteria and enough court space to handle cases expeditiously.

Making Smart Bets 

As just one example, among many other things, government policy is a matter of placing bets on the future. Will we be better off if interest rates rise or fall? Will a get-tough policy or seeking cooperation make for a safer, more prosperous world? And so forth, endlessly on.

Stability

Corollary to consistency: stability. oscillation is the enemy of progress. The truth is a source of stability; without such an anchor, impulse will drive you in one direction after another, at the very least a waste of energy. There are many sources of stability, of course, but seeking to anchor yourself to the truth helps a lot.

Composability

This is just a fancy term for the idea that you can build on reality. The more stable your view is, the more others can depend on it to take the next step. If there’s a single reason for the monstrous success of science and technology since the Enlightenment, it is this. Once orbital dynamics were understood, committees didn’t have to convene or leaders to dictate how to launch rockets; we could proceed directly to the exploration and exploitation of space. 

Reality is as reality does. If you believe that the vaccine is more dangerous than the disease, reality doesn’t care how strongly you believe it or how many people agree; you can kill millions with a misguided policy. If you believe that you have a “dead country” when in fact it is as robust as it has ever been and more robust than any other, then your desperation will drive risky policies to revive it, doing more harm than good. If you believe that climate change is a hoax, you’re gambling with the habitability of the environment.

There is a reason for keeping hands off of information-gathering functions: you not only want the best data you can get, you want that data to be trusted. We joke about shooting the messenger, but having the temerity to accept bad news is the only way to ensure the credibility of your position.
