The 2024 Election Is Over

[update, December 2024: Obviously I couldn’t have been more wrong. But I’m not taking this down because, you know, humility?]

You can judge an election by how dramatically it changes minds in the aftermath, from uncertainty and speculation beforehand, to forehead-slapping and eye-rolling amazement that anyone thought it could have turned out otherwise. I’m putting my money down that 2024 will be one of those elections: this will be a historic defeat for the Republican Party, possibly enough to trigger a re-organization of America’s two-party configuration, in which bonafide conservatives and Republicans who haven’t fallen under the spell of Trump — Republicans without a home, in other words — finally tire enough of being captive to his cult to break ranks and form a new party.

But I’m getting ahead of myself. Why will this election be such a surprise? Because it will unveil any number of should-be-obvious realities:

  • Donald Trump is increasingly incoherent and enfeebled, which will become undeniable when he proves too arrogant to avoid getting on a debate stage with Joe Biden.
  • Onrushing dementia aside, Trump 2024 is a downgrade from previous versions in so many respects: more caustic, more aggrieved, more openly authoritarian, more cruel, more flagrantly himself. The closer we get to the election, the clearer it will become that he’s a rotting albatross around the neck of the Republican Party.
  • Dementia and toxicity aside, Trump’s trials will increasingly weigh on his viability even without verdicts in the most important ones.
  • Joe Biden has a story to tell, and if his State Of The Union speech is any indicator, he’s ready to tell it. It’s one thing for voters to signal disgruntlement in polls more than half a year from voting, but what is the trend going to be as they actually start focusing on the fact that the country is, well, in the hands of adults?
  • Speaking of polling, what question do people think they’re answering when asked about their satisfaction with Biden, or their preferences in the upcoming election? How much are those answers infiltrated by concern about the general state of the country? The Presidency is a focus for popular discontent. But in the voting booth, the question is different: Whom do you want running the country? Could the choice be any clearer?
  • There is actual affection in this country for democracy and the institutions thereof, lingering though it may be. For anyone who holds such values close, the choice is clear — and, again, will only become clearer in the minds of voters as the election looms.
  • People — not just women — care about reproductive rights. Arizona has just come alive, and abortion will be on the ballot in Florida in the starkest terms: a six-week ban vs. a constitutional amendment securing reproductive freedom. How many other states will be voting too? And how many MAGA officeholders will be turned out over the issue?
  • I have a rule about impending disasters, derived from long experience, including Y2K: onrushing disasters that are anticipated in enough force and detail fall flat when the time comes. There is enough alarm in the nation over the prospect of a second Trump term to get people by the millions moving to forestall it.
  • Just on the simple matter of campaign management, Trump has jettisoned a wide swath of the Republican Party organization — and the work they have done to date — in favor of his loyalists. And, as David French has pointed out, Trump can’t help making the election all about himself — a strategic blunder when running against an incumbent.

What does Trump have to counterweight these realities? His inflamed sense of grievance strikes a chord with some segments of the population, to be sure, but it seems increasingly clear that his manner of keeping them onboard is a boat anchor dragging on the the task of reaching others. He has cast the spell of the Strong Leader over a wide swath of the electorate. But how is that segment supposed to grow?

I’m sure that these and many other factors will be obvious in retrospect — so obvious, in fact, that there will much hand-wringing over a very interesting question: what is wrong with our information systems that we could miss something so obvious? Why is polling so poor at providing relevant data? Why did the ongoing monsoon of “news” about the antics of Trump so thoroughly drown the through-line, the high-order bit, the thing we actually care about?

It’s an interesting question to ponder about our information systems as a whole, but it assumes practical importance when you ponder its effect on the state of our politics: the remaking of the Republican Party in Trump’s image — in particular its running roughshod over conservatives of principle and integrity — gets its juice from his perceived invincibility. When that illusion burns up like a magician’s flame effect, leaving a hollowed-out Republican Party behind, and a generation of pols blinking their eyes in the daylight, what then?
