The Joy of Paradox

Paradox is not contradiction, and it’s especially not hypocrisy.

The Joy of Paradox

I love paradox. Isn’t that perverse? The word comes with discomfort, the tension between two ideas that take effort to reconcile: the Zen koans of everyday life. Yet there’s an energy there that becomes quite addictive. For those who love the feeling of Understanding The World, few things can compare.

Looking back (and around, today), the popular disdain for “intellectuals” was and is hilarious. The notion that there must be something wrong with anyone who would devote any attention at all to ideas when there is actual reality to engage with, was and is an evergreen source of contempt among the practical folk.

What’s hilarious about that, of course, is that it’s like fish dissing water. Ideas are the very coin of the realm for anyone dealing with the world or communicating with their fellows. They’re what we use to wrangle reality in our heads. It’s not that there are some who deal in ideas and others who have no use for them; it’s that everyone deals with them all day long. The only difference is whether they realize that, or turn their backs on it.

What happens when ideas or principles collide?

Ambiguity vs. Ambivalence

As a good neoliberal, I hold two firm opinions about capitalism and the free market. It is the most efficient system for organizing economic activity ever devised, a relentless engine of prosperity. And also, it is a machine that discounts humanity and entrenches advantage, engendering cycles of both poverty and wealth to an extent that is simply oppressive in the absence of mitigating action. As my uncle, a staunch capitalist, used to say, “Them as has, gets.”

Yes, these two positions are paradoxical — but they’re not contradictory. Reasonable people can hold both of them at the same time; in fact, it seems obvious to me that reasonable people should hold both of them, and firmly, because both of them are true. But how much sound and fury comes directly and exclusively from the assumption that if one is true, the other can’t be? How much betrayal felt by those committed to one side when one of their own says anything sympathetic to the other? These are symptoms of one thing only: the instinct that says if two ideas are in tension, one of them must be wrong, i.e., you have to choose, to take sides. Politics arranges itself on a spectrum according to allegiance to these two positions, from libertarians firmly and exclusively planted at one end, all the way to utopian ideals of collective ownership at the other. The very idea of compromise becomes associated with dilution, of abandoning commitment.

The inability to tolerate paradox has another aspect: the image of the namby-pamby liberal who can’t make up his or her mind in the face of a difficult choice, who wants to have it both ways and so winds up with a kind of mental mush. But the error there is trying to resolve the paradox by combining the opposing poles rather than leaving them distinct and keeping them both vividly in mind. It’s the difference between ambiguity and ambivalence: the former denotes an unclear distinction; the latter, a strong commitment to both sides — literally, ambi-valent. The two couldn’t be more different (as it were).

What does this mean in practice when it comes to managing a market-based economy?

  • recognize market-distorting subsidies when you see them (ag, water)
  • make investments, not subsidies
  • remove competition over the public good, asserting a minimum standard of behavior for the market to work within (mileage standards for cars)
  • use the marketplace to force change: carbon taxes, realistic water prices, jump-starting “good” directions, e.g. renewables
  • act explicitly to mitigate the accumulation of wealth
  • resist the influence of money on politics
  • craft a firm social safety net, a minimum standard of living below which nobody need drop
  • Meditation can be quite paradoxical — sometimes we get what we want when we stop wanting it
  • We can’t predict the weather, but we can get a handle on the climate: https://www.quantamagazine.org/the-climate-change-paradox-20250915/

To the extent that political dialogue in the US is sincere, and centers around different people’s perspective on this basic issue,** polarization can be described as entrenchment at one side or the other of this paradox, with free-marketers ever more dismissive of social concerns, and vice versa. But I would like to suggest that embracing a paradox like this is actually the way out of the bind we find ourselves in.

** Yes, I realize that this may be a naive assumption. It assumes that American politics hasn’t devolved into a battle of power and naked self-interest. I suppose this is the namby-pamby liberal in me coming out.
