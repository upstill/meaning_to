The Problem with Commun(al)ism

A cold look at collectivist systems turns up a better way to think about state intervention.

The Problem with Commun(al)ism

It’s a crucial part of the divide between Left and Right, how much the state should provide resources on behalf of everyone. Even in America, despite its Everyone For Themselves ethic, most everyone agrees that it is the state’s business to provide for national defense, policing, firefighting, roads and schools. But there are well-gnawed bones of contention over providing for the disadvantaged, climate-change mitigation, and “socialized” access to medical care. 

These seem like issues that begin and end with values like personal responsibility vs. compassion for those of straitened circumstances. People also differ on how seriously to take the free-rider problem of beneficiaries unfairly gaming the system. In such a battle of values, it’s hard to envision any productive dialogue and easy to despair of making any progress. But there’s a way to put such discussions on a much more practical footing. It goes back to a realization I had back in 9th Grade, when I assigned myself to write a paper for English class on The Communist Manifesto. 

This was back in the Sixties. The Red Scare of the Fifties was over, but with America embroiled in Vietnam — ostensibly to resist the hegemonious impulses of the USSR and Red China — Communism itself (as distinct from the dictatorial predilections of its nominal practitioners) was still the preeminent bête noire of the West. I wanted to figure out what the deal really was, so I picked up Karl Marx’s founding document.

Marx serves up plenty of socio-historical analysis, which 14-year-old me was eminently unqualified to judge and 69-year-old me can happily live without. Not so, though, the basic idea for a radical model of how a society should manage who gets what:

From each according to his ability, to each according to his needs.

In other words, the production of labor should be pooled and then distributed among the population according to need.

The problems with that proposition may seem self-evident, because human nature. But looking underneath the obvious turns up a tool of thought that’s more than a little useful when considering many other policies for redistributing resources.

Consider the mathematics involved — or rather, simple arithmetic. 

Ideology and idealism aside, you can really learn a lot by looking closely at this proposition.

This is a manifesto for gaming the system. If I collectivize with three others, my contributions get me 1/4 back. With 10 people total, I get back only 10% of my production. Conversely, every unit I take out of the system only costs my share of the system a quarter or a tenth of that unit.

Under such a distribution scheme, it’s easier to get resources by milking the output than by contributing to the input. And the problem only gets worse as the pool of people increases. At the level of countries, the motivation to contribute to (or not to game) goes to zero, as does the cost to me of what I take out.

The only thing standing in the way of this insidious dynamic is the individual’s commitment to making it work — at the expense of his/her own interests. As an individual, you’re weighing your belief in sharing against the temptation for free-riding. And as the number of people in the system gets bigger, you have to get REALLY idealistic.

But it’s worse than that. Not only do you as an individual have to resist that temptation, in fealty to your fellow humans, you have to trust that everyone else is doing the same. Free riders and cheaters will be very conspicuous. How do you maintain your ideals when others are so richly rewarded for not doing so? This is exactly what happened in Soviet Russia, where party apparatchiks lorded over the commoners.

I should mention that such pooling of resources isn’t always a bad idea. This is exactly the idea behind insurance: people pool their money against some tragedy, paying out to the unlucky few it falls to. But insurance has systemic resistance to unchecked greed, so it’s harder to cheat. But even there, people do cheat: who resists the temptation to pad their insurance claims? It’s not just communist social organization that has this basic flaw.
