Why Dieting is Hard…also Climate Change…also Racism

An act just is different from a habit. A decision just is different from a policy.

Why Dieting is Hard…also Climate Change…also Racism

Ever wonder why dieting is such a struggle? Why is it so difficult to attack climate change as a society? Why has it been so hard to get people to wear masks against the coronavirus? Each of these challenges has its own distinct features, of course, but there’s a common thread that runs between them — and many other issues that people face, both individually and collectively. And without coming to grips with that aspect, solutions will remain elusive, and we’ll stay frustrated because we don’t understand why — kind of like being hounded by invisible spirits.

To put it simply, that thread is a failure to understand, respect, and successfully cope with the difference between the individual and the collective. A decision vs. a policy. An act vs. a habit.

What is dieting? It’s long-term behavior (eating less, or better) directed at long-term goals (improved health, weight loss, etc.). But how is dieting practiced? As countless individual decisions of how much to eat this time. The key thing to realize is that any one of those individual decisions/acts has virtually no bearing on the long-term goal. When I am tempted by a fabulous piece of chocolate cake, it literally doesn’t matter whether I eat it or not. By itself, it has no measurable impact on the success of the diet plan I swore to on New Year’s Day. A long succession of such decisions definitely matters, but the individual act does not, in a very literal sense. And the gap between the two is a chasm into which the best-laid plans fall.

What I’m getting at is a blind spot in our worldview: an assumption that if we want the general outcome, then of course we’ll behave ourselves in the day-to-day. In other words, we walk around grindingly unaware that they’re not the same thing at all, and this confusion brings endless frustration at all levels of civilization from the personal to the global.

(If you can stomach a little math, this distinction is enshrined forcefully and starkly in the theory of sets, distinguishing between a member (individual), and a set, a collection of members. At a basic level members and sets are different logical types. They are thought of in different ways and subject to different treatment. Never the twain shall meet.)

An item is different from a collection. A decision is different from a policy. An act is different from a habit.

The struggle to keep to a diet is nothing more and nothing less than the struggle — mental, emotional and behavioral — to forge a link between the two, to make the act reflective of the policy. The reason it’s hard is that, at a basic level, no such link exists; we get frustrated and confused because we think the linkage is obvious, that we shouldn’t want to eat the chocolate cake if we want to lose weight. There we are, struggling with the gap between the two, with no idea that there even is a gap.

There are exceptions to this sad picture, and they’re worth a look because they highlight the issue. People do make plans, which amount to translating a longer-term goal (what I want out of life: career, education, budgets) into finer-grained steps (actions this year, or day-to-day). Once the plan is in place, the steps become part of a framework that helps them along more than, say, reading this book, then that book, and hoping they add up to something.

The case of budgeting is interesting because it breaks the broad goal of solvency or saving into categories, and those categories into small (day-to-day), salient boundaries, where overstepping the budget in a small way feels more like a violation of the grand plan, in a way that just buying that sticky bun in the absence of multilevel planning would not. Budgeting doesn’t always work (some people and circumstances — and budgets — are more amenable to success than others), but it is a lever that improves the odds of success.

Dieting is hard because it should be hard. There are workarounds in the budgeting sense (Weight Watchers, meal plan services, etc.), but the temptation to cheat is always there because a single act of cheating just doesn’t, as a matter of fact, affect the overall goal. The fact that we sometimes achieve it is the surprising thing, the unexpected thing, the thing that should be celebrated when it works rather than getting mad or frustrated or judgy when someone has difficulty linking the two.

It’s the same deal with procrastination. Why should I do this thing today? What difference does it make if I do it tomorrow instead? Well, no difference, actually. It’s only the aggregate of tomorrows that makes any real difference. Again with smoking cessation: that next cigarette has literally no long-term impact whatsoever. How do sensible people wind up with catastrophic credit-card debt? One purchase at a time.

Only in the aggregate…

Why has it been such a struggle to get Americans to take masks and social distancing seriously during the pandemic? After all, the numbers are clear, and the US has half a million corpses to show for it. But when you walk into a crowded store without a mask, the probability of a dire outcome is actually pretty low, kind of like playing Russian roulette with a thousand-chamber gun. You as an individual would have to play a lot of times for disaster to ensue — but that’s the level at which society operates, and suffers: it takes billions of careless acts produce that many deaths. We have largely failed as a society to make the requisite link between the individual decision and the outcomes at a societal level.

Big box stores and suburban malls have been hollowing out downtowns not because WalMart improves the local environment, but because there is just no salient connection between going to WalMart today for price and convenience, and the downstream consequences. Nobody voted for WalMart over Main Street, but their individual decisions had that collective outcome.

Heartland America hates jobs being shipped abroad, but who chooses the more expensive domestically manufactured item over the cheap Chinese knockoff? People complain about air travel being uncomfortable but they don’t think of the cumulative impact on quality from countless immediate decisions on price. Were they voting for a shitty travel experience? No, but that was the long-term outcome of such decisions over decades. Does anyone want a crisis in the availability and affordability of housing in the US? No, but they made millions of small decisions — in zoning, in regulations, in NIMBY behavior — each perfectly reasonable at a local level, and each making a negligible contribution to the end result.

Some acts get their meaning only at the level of statistics, which is to say, in the aggregate. That’s one reason why racism is so hard to eradicate. Suppose society managed to quell all vestiges of overt racism: perfect racial harmony on the surface. If a recruiter is interviewing job candidates and picks the white candidate over the Black one, it’s logically impossible to call that out as racist, assuming the absence of other signals. But if that recruiter, or their company as a whole, or the society in general show a pattern of exclusion and preference, then there’s a problem. This is what I fear is the hardest part of systemic racism: a pattern of oppression that emerges from a multitude of individually reasonable decisions even without expressed or even conscious hostility. If my life and my prospects were limited by something so diffuse, something so lacking in accountability, then I would be consumed by rage. And when there’s no obvious, tangible target of blame, I would acquire a hypersensitivity to anything that even looks like it has a tinge of racism. Sound familiar?

So there you have it: a broad range of human struggles, frustrations and outright anguish, just from falling into the gap between the individual and the collective. But what can be done about it?

A Couple of Ideas

Habit formation has been getting some love in the self-help world of late, which is interesting because habits are all about behavior in the aggregate. Of course, a habit is still made up of individual acts, so acquiring one is always going to be a challenge, but it’s encouraging to see habits addressed per se. James Clear’s book Atomic Habits has some clever hacks for working at the habit level, including:

  • starting small, with outright trivial behaviors, for example beginning on a habit of working out by just showing up at the gym for five minutes;
  • making a behavior more automatic by associating it with recurring triggers in the environment; and
  • reconceptualizing each individual act of X from a make-or-break success or failure, to “a vote for yourself as the kind of person who does X”.

For behaviors on a more public level, I notice that a good chunk of moral strictures are designed to conjure up link between the act and the habit, between the decision and the policy. There are obvious strictures based on the Golden Rule (“Act as you would be treated”) that address behaviors where every act is a violation (like the Commandments against killing, cheating, stealing and lying). But others are driven by aggregate-driven questions like “What if everybody did it?” (Even an atheist has to admit: you can’t beat moral injunctions backed by Divine authority for changing behavior at the individual level.)

Why do we recycle? Not because anyone’s household garbage has the slightest global impact. We do it either because it feels wasteful, or because we think everyone should do it. We’ve somehow forged that moral link between our behavior and everyone’s behavior.

Why is such shame attached to driving impaired, and why do we feel so wrong when we drive around without seatbelts? Not because the odds are very high of getting into trouble —once. It’s because if we do it repeatedly, or if many people do it, then there will be consequences. Somehow, and not very long ago, the society managed to make the connection salient and instill that ethic.

But there are plenty of public problems whose solution still eludes us, starting with that classic economic conundrum, the “tragedy of the commons”. The commons in question is a village commons, a tract of land open to grazing, shared by all the villagers: if everyone grazes it, it’s guaranteed to be over-grazed and finally exhausted. But it’s in every individual’s obvious interest to graze it because a piece of a failing resource is still better than nothing. Individually rational actions produce complete collective destruction.

The biggest commons of all is the natural world and its resources — depletion of fisheries being a pointed example. Exploitation of natural resources is an especially thorny commons problem because the consequences are so backloaded: when people first arrived in a place there was every reason to make the most of nature’s offerings and no reason not to. It’s only now, as the earth fills up, our lifestyles luxuriate and we realize that resources are finite, that stewardship has become an issue. I say the problem is backloaded because it’s not like we’re drawing down a bank account and trying to stay solvent. It’s that natural systems are subject to collapse: there may be a tipping point, beyond which the world plunges into disaster. That’s the crucial thing about climate change: once enough glaciers and ice caps melt, and tundra thaws, and the ocean reaches carbon saturation, we may have set off a downward spiral that we can’t even slow, much less reverse.

But however catastrophic, it’s still a commons problem, and we still don’t have either the will or the wisdom to connect individual behavior to global outcomes with any tools more effective than preaching and coaching.

This may be a harsh note to end on, but as you can see, all I have in hand is a highlighter. Individuals have fierce trouble accounting for the aggregate effects of their individual acts not because they’re selfishness, or unenlightened, or shortsighted, or otherwise flawed of character. We struggle with it because it is a hard problem in and of itself, and we should at least start addressing it as such. I don’t have any magic solutions, but I do submit that a good first step is to stop barking up the wrong tree.
