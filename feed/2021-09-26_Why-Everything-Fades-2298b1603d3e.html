Why Everything Fades

Look at you. You’re swimming in miracles, and they barely register:

  • You carry an all-knowing supercomputer in your pocket that connects you instantly across the world.
  • You have an unlimited selection of foods and goods from everywhere, and you have the means to buy them.
  • Your odds of dying from any disease that responds to vaccines, antiseptics or antibiotics are vanishingly small, and if you have a toothache, you don’t resign yourself to debilitating torture for weeks or months; you can make it vanish in an afternoon.
  • You have a car that will take you further in an hour than anyone could travel in a week just a few centuries ago, and if you want to go further you will, as Louis C.K. has pointed out, “sit in a chair and fly through the air, like a god!” The last time you flew, did you even look out the window?

Your plans and goals were all-consuming — when they were in the future. That next job, that big raise, that nice new car, that college admission, that big project, that exotic locale beckoning, that romantic partner who would make it all good. They loomed over your life and consumed your attention — then. And when you crossed the finish line, they were exciting…for a while. A very brief while. Now that you’ve tossed them on your pile of achievements, what is left of them?

And it’s not just you; the same is true for everyone, all the time. What’s wrong with us?

There is a key to this mystery. It’s rooted in basic sensory physiology, the wiring by which we take in the world. And there’s no better place to get insight into perception than looking in the eye, the human visual system.

How does anything register, visually?

How do you think vision works? The obvious, default model is that light gets projected on the back of the eyeball, where some neural circuitry picks it up and transfers the image to the brain, much like a digital camera. But the truth is far more interesting.

Consider the individual photoreceptor, one of the rods and cones at the back of the eye. When photons hit a receptor, it converts the light into a neural signal headed for the brain. If those signals were scaled to the force of the light (bright lights producing strong signals, dim lights weak ones) then you would have something akin to a video signal. But that’s not how it works.

Converting light into an electric charge involves chemistry: each cell has a stockpile of ions which give up their charge to fire the signal when struck by light. Once an ion discharges, it is depleted and needs to be restored, and that process can only go so fast. When the retina is being bombarded by bright light, more ions get depleted and the cell loses its sensitivity. Conversely, under low-light conditions there are plenty of ions available and the cell becomes more sensitive.

Middle-school biology teaches us that opening and closing the pupil is the whole story about dealing with different levels of illumination, and it is — within very strict limits. But this neurochemical accommodation is far more important in enabling our eyes to respond to a mind-boggling range of conditions. From a moonlit cave at midnight to a ski slope at noon, our visual system accommodates a range of brightnesses well into the billions. We do notice the difference between the worst extremes, if we have to put on sunglasses or wait to “get used to the dark”. However, there is a vast middle range over which are blissfully unaware of differences in overall brightness as they range wildly.

We are far more sensitive to changing conditions than to the steady state: remember blinking when stepping out into the sunlight. When a change hits, we become vividly aware, but then adaptation sets in and we head back toward a middling response. When I shave my beard off, I can feel air currents convecting up from my chest. Every little breeze announces itself with voluptuous clarity. But before long, my face is no more sensitive to such changes than my forearm. My baseline level of response is restored.

The importance of change to our perception cannot be overstated. Why do you think emergency signals blink and alarms modulate in sound? If we cared about absolute value, wouldn’t we just set them as bright/loud as possible and be done with it? Instead, we exploit sensitivity to change.

This is why movies which are just one heart-pounding scene after another, and music that’s relentlessly loud/aggressive, seem so dull: they’re literally monotonous (“one tone”).

And this is why the most salient thing about most flights is not the miracle of flight, but the traffic to and from the airport; why the joy of that new car fades in short order; why lottery winners and paraplegics find their way back to their prior level of contentment after a year or so. It’s the change that counts; before long, perception returns to normal

But why on earth would we work that way, emphasizing newness over the way things are? After all, the objects in the world out there are defined by their continuity. Shouldn’t our reactions track that? But preferential sensitivity to change makes evolutionary sense. If you’re a defenseless hairy ape, and you’ve survived until this moment, where are threats and opportunities more likely to come from: the environment as it is, or from moment-to-moment developments? From all the boulders piled up on the hillside, or the one that’s come loose and rolling toward you? From the placid pool of water, or from the glint of motion underneath? From the thousands of cars that pass without incident, or the standout who is veering into your lane?

Once you put on these conceptual glasses, you can see human life as a struggle against the fact that our perceptions, our attention, our reactions, are based less on any fixed, objective reality, than on moment-to-moment change. Modulation.

But then there’s contrast…

There’s a second part of the story, also relating to visual physiology. When a photoreceptor fires, a signal heads for the visual cortex. But the signals passed thereby do not proceed in isolation from one another. Each “wire” is not an independent reporter. The retina also contains a lateral inhibitory network of interconnections among nearby cells, such that when a given cell fires, its signal inhibits the responsiveness of its neighbors.

This mechanism has two major effects. When nearby cells have a similar stimulation, they all inhibit each other. That reduces the visual response to flat fields like a uniformly painted wall. The second effect occurs near the boundaries of such fields. If the boundary between a brightly lit region and a dimmer region falls between two cells, the highly stimulated cell will inhibit its neighbor, making what’s darker appear darker still. Conversely, the dimly-lit cell will not be inhibiting the brightly-lit cell the way the latter’s other neighbors are, so its responsiveness is enhanced.

If this sounds confusing, you can see the effect for yourself:

By Aliwiki at French Wikipedia.

You’ll never convince yourself, but each bar in this image is precisely the same shade throughout. The righthand side of each band appears darker than its left because of inhibition across the boundary (and the left side is brightened by the absence of inhibition coming from its neighbors on the other side of the boundary). In other words, the visual system is biassed toward the contrast at each boundary.

Again, perfect evolutionary sense. Just as adaptation factors out absolute values of illumination and emphasizes temporal change, contrast sensitivity looks for spatial change, at edges and boundaries. If you’re trying to make out objects by their shape or texture, the boundaries are where the action is.

Now you can see why your left hand stuck into cold water will feel colder if the right hand is in warm water rather than the same cold water; why people who are poor relative to their surroundings feel poor, when they objectively have more infinitely more access to technology, nutrition, information and other resources than the kings of centuries past; why, in the memorable words of Gore Vidal, “It is not enough to win. Others must lose.”

What does this understanding buy?

You may question the connection between retinal physiology and, say, ongoing levels of happiness. Is this really how our minds process our larger experiences? What I’m proposing here is visual processing as a metaphor for how we deal with the world in general. Specifically, I’m offering the concrete facts of visual processing as a way of throwing light on the more abstract philosophical concerns of happiness, satisfaction, motivation, etc.

I’m inviting you to take this leap with me because 1) it helps to release the grip of the idea that we deal with things the way they are, rather than how they’re changing or how they compare to their neighbors; 2) from the standpoint of survival, we really are more interested in new threats and opportunities than in the steady state; and 3) minds really do seem to behave this way, heeding contrast and modulation over fixed facts. For example, the phenomenon of hedonic adaptation is well known: the pleasure of an ongoing stimulation diminishes over time, such that it requires increased levels of stimulation to achieve the same effect. So let’s go with the metaphor and see where it takes us, shall we?

With any such metaphor, it’s a reasonable question how far it goes. Adaptation reaches very far indeed, but there are exceptions. A very few phenomena fail to adapt out; it appears that animals of all kinds never get used to stress, for example. And there is such a thing as acquired sensitivity, in which the response to a stimulus increases with exposure. Some allergies develop over time — especially allergies to particular people.

Certainly absolute values do matter absolutely…sometimes. But change and contrast matter much more than we think, suggesting a number of lessons to keep handy in the adept thinker’s mental toolkit.

Don’t be surprised when things fade

Novelty is a force of nature in our perceptions and reactions, and nothing is ever as effective as when it is new. The failure to factor in that reality is responsible for a staggering amount of human disappointment.

How many lives are consumed with making more money, looking for that next step up that will finally let you relax? For paupers as for billionaires, the answer to the question “How much is enough?” is always “More”. Maybe 10, 20 percent more. That’ll be enough to stop striving. But when that day arrives — or not long after — the answer is still the same. It’s like the joke with the fool and the shampoo bottle: Lather, rinse, repeat, ad infinitum. If the phrase hedonic treadmill sounds like something hamsters get stuck on, there’s a reason.

When it comes to picking and prioritizing future achievements (goals), our forethought pictures them on first encounter, in all their novelty, without thinking about how they will look and feel when they are no longer new. This is why “the next shiny thing” is so seductive: it can’t help but stand out against the existing things that are no longer new.

Exploiting novelty

But the unique value of novelty can be exploited for good, too. You may be lusting after some new thing, juicing your imagination with visions of how it will transform your life. Instead of diving into a prolonged battle of acquisition, or piling up your credit card debt even further, why not figure out a way to enjoy the novelty of it without a long-term cost? Is there a way to borrow, rent or otherwise use that thing, and see how long the allure lasts?

If you are looking at the dessert menu, but calories are an issue, you can remember what novelty (and experience) tells you: the first bite of that dessert will be eye-rollingly awesome, but by the time you finish the whole thing you’ll barely be paying attention. For me, the first two or three bites is half the net effect of the whole thing, but with a fraction of the calorie load. Maybe share dessert? Or take a teensy serving in the first place? Then give it your full, rapt attention. Just don’t fall out of your chair in a swoon.

Variety is the spice of life

There’s an excellent word for any stimulation that remains constant over time: “monotonous” (literally, “one-tone”). Gratuitous variety can be a low-cost way of enhancing experience.

I attended university on a fairly large campus, with far-flung classrooms. Walking among them, I made a conscious decision to vary my route, not because one was intrinsically more scenic than another, but because it kept my attention engaged more brightly.

Sometimes we think we need an improvement or a replacement, when we really only need a change (modulation) that makes us appreciate anew what we’ve got. To pick one coarse example, a couple who are bored in their relationship could try taking separate vacations. The simple act of parting and then reuniting may be all it takes to get back on track. And how many people have derailed their lives in a midlife crisis out of a sense of monotony, when a smart application of contrast or modulation could have made everything better?

The moment of change is the hard part

When facing a major change in life — breaking up with a partner, changing careers, moving away, etc. — it’s the moment of change that gets all the attention: the disruption, the chaos, the uncertainty, the unadapted rawness of it. But that focus distracts from where you’re going to spend the vast bulk of your time: in the rest of your life after the change has settled and you have adapted to it. I find it reassuring to realize that if I just hold my nose and get through the transition, the rest will take care of itself.

The faster the change, the greater the impact

There’s an urban legend that you can boil a frog alive if you put it into a pot of cold water, then raise the temperature gradually. While that’s not true in reality, it does illustrate an important point: the faster the onset of a change, the more noticeable it will be — and vice versa.

If you’re going to make a change happen, and you want it to get the maximum attention, make it quickly. Conversely, gradual change is much more likely to sneak in unnoticed. Human relationships of all kinds often fall apart due to changes that no one would tolerate if they occurred suddenly. From lovers taking each other for granted, to toxic personalities revealing themselves gradually, dramatic changes can accrue one baby step at a time, finally reaching levels that seem intolerable to any bystander.

Conversely, you can introduce a big positive change in small increments. In his book Atomic Habits, James Clear suggests that it’s a mistake to just jump into a new habit in hopes of holding on until it takes root. His strategy is to start with the smallest possible version of the habit (e.g., driving to the gym without even getting out of your car), and increase the level of engagement gradually.

Notice contrast at work

When we look around, we see the world as a collection of objects. They persist through time, and you can move them from place to place without changing them. But the ideas with which we deal with the world are a different matter. The importance which we assign to any phenomenon thoroughly depends on the context in which we view it.

This is not the place for a treatise on duality and relativism. But for now, it’s enough to point out that we often think we’re dealing with some fixed, absolute fact of the world, when we’re willfully ignoring its need for a contrasting Other to give it meaning. There is no yin without yang. There is no figure without ground. There is no up without down. There is no evil without good. There is no long without short. There is no rich without poor. There is no smart without stupid. Keeping in mind the importance of contrast helps to remember that.

Find grounds for comparison

If adaptation makes the steady state invisible, we may become aware again by consciously comparing it against some other context. This is part of why an explicit practice of gratitude is so rewarding: it brings into mind (re-minds us) the ways in which things might be different. So does a recent memory of being sick; there’s nothing like sickness to make you appreciate health. And Sam Harris suggests a worthwhile exercise: recall the worst part of your day, and think about all the people in the world who would consider their prayers answered if they could trade places with you in that moment.

When our daughters were teenagers, the challenges of sex, drugs and alcohol suddenly loomed. The default track for parents is to push on abstinence as hard as possible, in hopes of holding off the inevitable as long as possible. Instead, we told them “Look, these things are real, but your brains and your emotions are still developing, so the smart move is to wait until you’re 18. After that, go nuts.” Framing the issue in terms of a contrast between now and later worked surprisingly well, at least in these two anecdotal cases.

In trying to communicate some state, it’s tempting to bring out strong, hyperbolic images in hopes of getting the point across (“You made me feel so so so so so bad”). But unless the communicatee has a similar experience to refer to, establishing some grounds for comparison helps to get the point across. This is one (not the only) reason why criticism is more effective when prefaced by noting some positive things a person has done.

Diversity pays

The movement for social justice in the US promotes diversity as a way to increase opportunities and participation for marginalized populations. That’s a laudable goal on its own, but there’s a more selfish reason to promote diversity of all kinds: keeping things fresh, vital, alive. Not only do we learn from different others, there’s no better way to understand who we are than by contrast with others of different origins, education, history, interests — quite apart from the simple fact that we can’t learn from people who are the same as us.

There’s no denying the very human tendency to sequester in tribes of all kinds, defined by what the members have in common. In fact, social cooperation goes a long way to explaining what made our species so spectacularly successful. And God knows how social media have weaponized tribes and tribalism in this century. So it’s especially important to keep in mind the associated hazards. In a landscape of sameness, not only does the lack of contrast make you unaware of where you’re at, but people become so sensitive to any difference that the smallest infraction can send whole populations off the rails. Sound familiar?

Coping with difference may feel like an onerous burden, but it’s good, and good for you.

Adopt an Alien Perspective

A.J. Jacobs has a post on Substack, The Benefits of Thinking Like An Alien which I think pertains here. The idea is simple: describe an aspect of your life as though you were an alien with no knowledge of life on earth. For example “I used a sharp metal object to remove some protein strands on my face but left those over my eyes untouched” to describe himself shaving.

I don’t expect anything in this essay to come as a great surprise. All of us are vaguely aware that change and contrast matter in our lives, that novelty has its own value, that it’s not what something is that matters so much as how it relates to its surroundings both temporally and spatially. But I doubt that most of us realize how important, yet invisible, these matters are as we go about making our plans, assessing our situations, navigating in the world — an importance that’s obscured by the seeming persistence of the things we deal with directly.

Anais Nin said “We don’t see things as they are. We see things as we are.” Using the visual system as a metaphor for how we deal with things goes a long way to explaining the astonishing realities listed up top. It also makes suggestions for a few life strategies:

  • Understanding that it’s not what you have that matters, it’s how you feel about what you have, especially in relation to its surroundings.
  • Redirecting attention away from things whose impact is going to fade with time and towards things of persistent value. For example, does it ever grow old having friends over for dinner?
  • Remembering that maintaining the gratification you get from things in your life is actually a problem worth paying attention to, and cultivating ways of refreshing your appreciation of them. Don’t marriages need attention in and of themselves?

Examining our lives with these tools in hand can be quite an eye-opening exercise. I’ve found the visual model to be a rich and entertaining way to cast a new light on life strategies and priorities — as well as to make better sense of these crazy humans (myself included) and their ways in the world. I hope it can do the same for you.
