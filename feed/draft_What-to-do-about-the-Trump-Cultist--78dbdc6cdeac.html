What to do about the Trump Cultist?

The pre-eminent fact about American politics today is, not to mince words, the hard core Trump cult: the roughly 30% of voters who are unpersuadably loyal to Donald Trump. They are the basis of the reign of terror that Trump holds over the Republican majority in Congress, simply because they can be counted upon to primary any Republican deemed apostate by The Man. They are the number below which his approval ratings will never sink no matter how dismal his record in office. And they present a nigh-insurmountable barrier to any Presidential challenger: when you start with a third of the electorate in your pocket no matter what, then you can get to a majority of the votes with only one quarter of the remainder.

Let’s take a moment to ponder this phenomenon: how such a remarkable thing happened, why it’s so stubborn, how to cope with it now, and what it means for the future, after Trump is gone. All of these are interesting questions because they’re unprecedented in my fairly elderly experience, and because they’re key contributors to the stress test that American democracy seems to be undergoing.

How did we get here?

The unswerving attachment to Donald Trump by the millions is without equal or precedent in my (fairly long) lifetime. I speak as a former William Buckley conservative who grew up in the South among people who would now be considered canonical Trump voters: frequently middling- to low-education (and proud of it), grumpy about racial equality, with visions of the Welfare Queen and the Red Menace dancing in their heads. George Wallace voters, businessman conservatives, veterans who thought the way soldiers were supposed to think (stalwart, disciplined, America-first). I had disagreements all along the way, but I also had some sense of their perspective, and always intuitively felt that they were persuadable, or at least there was a certain amount of respect going both ways. That there was real racial progress in the Sixties, the shift toward environmental protection over business as usual, and the turn against Nixon and the Vietnam War, would seem to validate that impression.

This time is different. 

Let‘s count the ways:

  • While the Right has always tried to make “liberal” more of an epithet than a label, the tone has gotten uniquely contemptuous.
  • Fealty to Trump entails surrendering if not backhanding all sorts of values that always seemed core to conservative ideology: the rule of law; the value of truth, or at least appearing truthful, over overt bullshit; expectations of financial probity for public officeholders; understanding and defending freedom of speech; revulsion at hypocrisy; expressing and upholding immutable standards of personal morality; aversion to Presidential over-reach; alarm at even the appearance of corruption; deference to science; hair-trigger sensitivity to any whiff of a police state; rejection of socialism; respect for institutional norms in general and in particular the independence of institutions like the Justice Department and the Federal Reserve. Imagine Barry Goldwater rationalizing the January 6 invasion of Congress!
  • The US has become more geographically polarized, with rural areas becoming more red and urban areas more blue, with the effect of firming up siloes as it becomes more unlikely that anyone will meet/talk to their political others and, conversely, are surrounded by people who agree with them.
  • Similarly, on the education front the gap in level of education has turned into a gulf. David Brooks points out that “[T]he academic gap between the affluent and less affluent is greater today than the achievement gap between white Americans and Black Americans in the final days of Jim Crow.” This kind of segregation will of course tend to divide along partisan lines, but more important, the resentment it breeds will also be a major driver for attachment to anyone who capitalizes on it.
  • News has become more strained along ideological lines. When broadcast news came over the air there was a strict regulatory regimen (the FCC’s Fairness Doctrine) dictating at least the appearance of non-partisanship and attention to different sides of an issue. Where does enthusiastic, joyfully committed loyalty come from

From the early days of the first Trump presidency, I actually thought that, shocking as it was that so many people had voted for such a creature, the truth on the ground as that Presidency played out would have a big impact on his level of support. In economic terms, I thought his support was elastic. I believe I said at the time that, soon enough, people would be begging for the crook

It didn’t take much polling experience before it became clear: about a third of the American electorate, the very core of Trump’s support, wasn’t going anywhere.

The most obvious consequence of that fact was electoral. When you’re running for President and you start with a third of the electorate in your pocket, the field is not just tilted, it’s nigh impossible: to get yourself over 50% of the votes, you only need a quarter of the remaining 2/3
