Trump supporters are a pack of deplorables

Hillary Clinton took withering flak during the 2016 presidential campaign for this statement:

You know, to just be grossly generalistic, you could put half of Trump’s supporters into what I call the basket of deplorables. Right? The racist, sexist, homophobic, xenophobic, Islamaphobic — you name it. And unfortunately there are people like that. And he has lifted them up.

It’s hard to argue with what she’s actually saying here, i.e., “who else are the racist, sexist, etc. people going to vote for?” Trump and his rhetoric give them a home.

The pattern under discussion thus has the features “Trump supporter” and “political deviant.” Political deviants are observable among Trump supporters. The Turn occurs with the reflex to tag all Trump supporters with the “political deviant” label — and basically everybody at the time fell all over themselves making that turn and blasting away that either Clinton was a horrible elitist or had made herself seem that way. 

But it’s absolutely untrue that Clinton herself had such a negative opinion of Trump supporters in general. Just read what she said immediately after that incendiary comment:

But the other basket — and I know this because I see friends from all over America here — I see friends from Florida and Georgia and South Carolina and Texas — as well as, you know, New York and California — but that other basket of people are people who feel that the government has let them down, the economy has let them down, nobody cares about them, nobody worries about what happens to their lives and their futures, and they’re just desperate for change. It doesn’t really even matter where it comes from. They don’t buy everything he says, but he seems to hold out some hope that their lives will be different. They won’t wake up and see their jobs disappear, lose a kid to heroin, feel like they’re in a dead-end. Those are people we have to understand and empathize with as well.

If The Turn didn’t actually cost Hillary Clinton the presidency, it certainly made a key contribution.

The police are a clear and present danger to Black men in America

The brutality of police killings of Black men in America is seared into the memory of anyone who’s seen the George Floyd video or tracked similar crimes in the news. The pattern associating Blackness (especially young, male Blackness) with atrocity is not to be denied.

As horrible as that reality is, and as much as it triggers justifiable fear and reanimates the sordid history of racism in America, nonetheless: you have to go through The Turn to reach the conclusion that police violence is an imminent threat to young Black males. Yes, absolutely, police violence is frequently visited upon them. But “threat” means a high risk that any given young Black man will be beaten down. Is the average Black man actually in danger of having it happen to them? Statistically, in a random group of 100,000 Black men, between 1.9 and 2.4 of them will be killed by police. (The corresponding figure non-Hispanic white males is between .6 and .7).

It’s important to put such figures into context. In a group of 100,000 Black men, how many will die of cancer? About 216— roughly 100 times more. In fact, the difference in cancer rate between white and Black men is more than 40 per 100,000. Black men are about 40 times more likely to die of cancer because they’re Black than to die at the hands of police because they’re Black.

More pertinently, what is the overall risk of dying by homicide, including all other contexts? According to DoJ figures, between 1980 and 2018, 27.8 Black people per 100,000 died of homicide. Only a fraction (1/10) of Black people are killed by police than by others.

So what is the point of stomping through this minefield of atrocity, feelings and triggers? If your sole focus is on protecting Black men from horrific death by focusing on official homicide, you’re being woefully distracted. The (much larger) race-based disparity in things like death from cancer and overall life expectancy is one thing. But even confining your attention to crime, the problem stretches far beyond police culpability. The truth is, law enforcement is grossly underserving the Black community, for reasons having to do with historical racism and an ongoing suspicion held by that community. We need to be working to heal the rift between the two, focusing on building trust in the community and, in general, moving police culture from a samurai-like Guardian model to a Servant model. It is possible to work in that direction if you’ve decided that the police are the enemy, but you might find it easier going if you back up and resist that Turn.
