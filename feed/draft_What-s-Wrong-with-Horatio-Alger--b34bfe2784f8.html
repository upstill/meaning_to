What’s Wrong with Horatio Alger?

The grip of the Horatio Alger myth depends on a mind virus that we’re all prey to.

What’s Wrong with Horatio Alger?

It’s hard to exaggerate the hold of Horatio Alger on the American mind. If American culture has a foundational story, it’s that of America as the land of opportunity: with enough pluck, grit, hard work and sterling character, you’re bound to succeed. This story became popularized in a long series of novels by Horatio Alger featuring stalwart boys rising above challenging circumstances to ascend to middle class respectability.

Some people take that story at face value, sorting others into the valiant and the poor. Other people take it as a historical artifact and a quaint bundle of naiveté. Still others see active evil in its implication that the unsuccessful have no one else to blame for their failures. But all these people should think again. The problem with the Horatio Alger narrative isn’t cultural, or moral, but logical. There’s a critical flaw in the logic at hand, a flaw that throws a cold light on a whole domain of human cognition. 

To spot that flaw, consider a lesser tenet of the American Ethos, the idea that “Anyone can become President”. While that’s strictly true (there’s theoretically nothing to stop any non-criminal of sufficient age from ascending to the White House), it’s kind of beside the point, because it obviously doesn’t follow that “Everyone can become President.”

That difference is so obvious that it hardly bears mentioning, but it’s that exact distinction that breaks the Horatio Alger paradigm. It’s “Anyone can succeed if…” vs. “Everyone will succeed if…” Pluck and hard work may be necessary for success, but they don’t necessarily ensure it.

To see this, consider a key feature of the actual books of Horatio Alger that gets short shrift in the ideal of the Self-Made Man(/Woman/Boy): the appearance of a “mysterious stranger” or a “worldly but warmhearted patron” who recognizes the hero’s potential and provides a timely boost to his prospects. In reality, pluck, hard work, etc. are rarely enough. They need to be met with some kind of assist from the outside world, whether it’s happening to hear about a job opportunity, a personal connection, someone who takes a person under their wing, or a host of other factors that are impossible to predict or control via personal choices — starting with choice of parents.

There’s an old saw that comes in handy here: “Luck is what happens when preparation meets opportunity.” Removing the irony connects it better with Horatio Alger: “Success is what happens when preparation meets opportunity.” Preparation is all well and good, and Horatio Alger is fine for such preparation. But what if that opportunity never arrives? What if it’s blocked by, for example, medical damage arising from straitened origins? What if you didn’t go to school with future titans of industry? What if opportunities are foreshortened by one’s skin color, or dragged down by culture gaps? Viewing people’s ongoing economic struggles through this other lens beyond personal character issues makes lack of success much more understandable.

The smaller point here — not small at all — is that a person’s character isn’t necessarily at fault for their lack of achievement, given the host of factors that go into the mysterious “opportunity”, including lucking into the requisite opportunity. But the larger point, what this essay is really about, is the logical flaw in Horatio Alger, one that undermines human thinking across the board. Another example will bring that into focus.

Islam and Terrorism

It’s a bit dated, but let’s go back to a syllogism guaranteed to raise hackles:

  1. Dangerous terrorists are generally Muslim.
  2. Therefore, Muslims are a suspect, if not hostile, population.

Politically, this is a pretty important syllogism, and the arguments against it usually boil down to two: 1) it’s factually incorrect, i.e., terrorism, properly defined, is more a function of white nationalism — at least in the US; and 2) even if a steady parade of terrorist actions are committed by Jihadists, it’s unfair, prejudicial, and xenophobic to tar all Muslims with that brush. Treated thus, the argument descends to arguing over the definition of terrorism, what period of time to attend to, pulling intolerant/aggressive quotes from the Q’uran, etc. Not very helpful.

But here again, there’s a logical flaw in the argument that trumps matters of fact and fairness. Assume for the sake of argument that all terrorists really are Muslim. Therefore one could, in theory, eliminate terrorism by eliminating contact with Muslims. But that’s all. It does not tell you that Muslims are in fact a dangerous population, for the simple reason that terrorists are so few in number. That is, even if all Jihadists are Muslim, they could be a vanishingly small exception in an overwhelmingly benign population. 

Muslim terrorists are observable. We have observed them, no doubt. Maybe even all terrorists are Muslim. But that doesn’t mean terrorism is predictable among Muslims. No matter how terrified you are, picking a Muslim at random and finding a terrorist is about as likely as winning the lottery. 

Horatio Alger may be right in that success is observable among people who work hard, etc. It may even be that everyone successful shares a certain set of character traits. But that doesn’t mean that everyone who shares that set of traits will be a success.

The Turn

This extra step in thinking, going from the observable to the predictable, is what I call “The Turn”. And it trips us up not only with regard to terrorism and success, but in a wide variety of situations across personal judgement and politics. It’s a deeply ingrained flaw in human thinking that’s insidious and commonplace.

The Turn happens because we have brains that use patterns to make sense of the world. For example, suppose you’re seeking a restroom in a commercial building. You find one near the elevator, but it’s for the wrong gender. Do you pass it by and start your search anew, as if you have learned nothing? Of course not. You start looking for the other gender in a similar position to the first one with respect to the elevator, perhaps on the other side of it, maybe even directly above it on the next floor. You can generally count on restrooms being found either together, or in some kind of symmetry.

We have a set of patterns for the way restrooms are organized in buildings, suggesting how they are arranged with respect to each other. We also have a meta-pattern of symmetry that’s useful as well.

Learning isn’t just a matter of memorizing facts; we notice and store patterns — for reasons tightly linked to survival. If eating this plant makes you sick, having that plant and the reaction associated in a pattern helps you avoid future harm. If you learn that baby bears are likely to be near a grumpy mama bear, you can avoid a deadly confrontation. And so on.

When, in the course of human events, elements of a pattern show up, we recall the pattern. If the pattern includes elements not in evidence, we infer that those elements are also present, perhaps hidden. In other words, we associate items that appear together in our patterns. If we see the back of someone’s head we recall the pattern for a human figure, and infer that there’s a face on the other side of it. We get into a car and know where to look for features like glove compartment, seat belt, turn signal, etc. We recognize a book by its general appearance (hand-holdable size, rectilinear shape, prominent title and cover matter) and know to expect a stack of pages with writing on them that flows from front to back, where each letter, each word, each sentence is a pattern defined by the language the book is written in. We know that speaking sharply to our boss is likely to come bundled with certain consequences sooner or later. 

One of the more important roles of patterns is the surprise reaction to having a pattern violated, because surprising circumstances are often where threats and opportunities lie. If your friend turns around and has a black eye, that becomes the most important thing in the room. If your boss responds to your criticism with warm equanimity, you’ll lose sleep that night wondering what that means.

Why Does Drug Use Seem Bad?

Sure enough, the wages of drug abuse are often visible, from crack babies to wasted meth heads to an epidemic of overdose deaths. Does that mean drug use predictably or dependably leads to such outcomes? Not at all. Not only are there more- and less-harmful drugs, but there are people who are more- and less-able to navigate those waters.

Nonetheless, a pattern took hold of the popular mind, beginning in the 70’s with Richard Nixon’s War On Drugs and continuing with Nancy Reagan’s Just Say No campaign and continuing today with the still-popular D.A.R.E. program.

The pattern in question associates two things: 1) the category “illegal drugs”, and 2) dire consequences, ranging from brain damage to addiction to social incompetence and family destruction. Those terms are resoundingly vague, but that doesn’t stop people from clutching that association with every ounce of their being. In fact, we have a meta-pattern that we overlay on the association: cause-and-effect: use of drugs causes these dire consequences.

The Drugs Are Bad pattern was never logically viable: The Turn goes from the obvious (some people have trouble handling mind-altering substances) to the unsupported (those things are dangerous in themselves) by passing right over the possible hordes who deal with them with no problem and in fact may derive great benefit. Fortunately, the hold on minds by this particular Turn is loosening rapidly: fatigue with the War On Drugs is finally setting in, and a number of the substances in the government’s Most Dire bin (in the US, the FDA’s schedule 1 for drugs with “no medical use and greatest potential for addiction”) are showing extraordinary promise, e.g. the use of MDMA (Ecstasy or Molly) to treat PTSD, or psilocybin (magic mushrooms) in treatment for addiction and end-of-life anxiety.

Science vs. Scientism

In recent centuries, Science has accumulated a glorious history of gleaning dependable (never say “true”) information about the world. Beginning with the Greeks and truly exploding with the Enlightenment, a torrent of knowledge has poured out of the scientific project: Pythagoras’s Theorem, Newton’s laws of motion and thermodynamics, Galileo’s cosmology, Maxwell’s equations, genetics, natural selection, epidemiology, electronics, etc., ad nauseum. The most prominent features of modern life — extended lifespan, technological magic, unprecedented material comforts — derive directly from knowledge accumulated scientifically. The pattern associating Science with knowledge seems irresistible.

But does that mean valid knowledge must be scientific? Will science help discern values? What does it tell about spiritual/mystical experience? Are there no other, complementary systems that provide, e.g., metaphorical understanding of life’s big questions? What about knowledge honed after generations without number by indigenous peoples? Sure, they can be empirically investigated, but, coming not from the lab but rather (often) other races or colors, they face an uphill battle to adoption.

There is a population of people to whom scientific knowledge, gained through a process of formulating hypotheses and putting them through rigorous testing, is the only kind of knowledge that matters. In other words, only knowledge that can be tested, and tested in the standard fashion, counts as knowledge at all. In making the leap from the breathtaking effectiveness of science for building knowledge, to the belief that no other kind of knowledge counts, such people are going from Science to Scientism.

What kinds of understanding does Scientism leave out? That question deserves a whole essay of its own, but just think about how the scientific method would work on:

  • spirituality: the sense that we are connected to something larger;
  • wisdom: defined as an inchoate belief, derived from experience, that has no means of proof;
  • values: the sense people have of what matters to them;
  • meaning: every response we have to a situation beyond cold hard facts;
  • metaphor: our gift for using knowledge about one thing to shed light on another. How unscientific can you get?

To dismiss everything that can’t be put under the reductionist, empirical microscope of science is Scientism. And the root of Scientism is starting with the pattern associating Science with firm knowledge, then taking The Turn, associating firm knowledge only with Science.

Interestingly, the belief that all knowledge proceeds from science is itself 100% unscientific: untestable, unfalsifiable, it’s nothing but a judgment derived — illogically — from experience. Moreover, scientism is itself refuted, for the quintessentially scientific domain of mathematics, by Gödel’s Incompleteness Theorem, which showed that no system built from a set of axioms that is internally consistent can be used to prove all true theorems in that system. In other words, if a (mathematical) system is consistent, then it cannot be complete (represent all truths). It must leave something out.

The scientistic mind has been hijacked by The Turn.

Free-market Economics

Ever since Adam Smith’s The Wealth of Nations, the North Star of American economic policy has been free enterprise: the ideal that when people are rational actors, and when they each pursue their own self-interest in a fair marketplace, the “invisible hand” of competition optimizes productivity and wealth.

This clear and clean story is, of course, muddied by issues like the licentious extraction of resources, failure to assign the cost of externalities, colonialist and racist exploitation, and the way that money and power just seem to go together so that, in my uncle’s immortal phrase, “them as has, gets.” Still, America has done very well for itself these last few centuries. So let’s assume for the sake of argument that free-market economics strongly support an economically vigorous nation.

Thus, the pattern at hand associates free-market economics with prosperity, however loosely. In this case, one aspect of The Turn is the idea that “the freer the market the better it works”: every impediment removed from the market’s machinery tunes its performance higher. That’s the libertarian ideal, and it has gotten plenty of pushback. But I want to highlight another aspect of The Turn here: the implication that a free market is all you need for a healthy society.

Put it like that and you’ll hardly find anyone to disagree; everyone has their own pet feature(s) of a “just” society, whether it’s religion, healthy communities, family, patriotism, equal opportunity or any other ancillary value. It’s a tenet of liberalism that these elements need to be nurtured directly, often with state support, because they’re orthogonal if not contradictory to the general materialistic impetus of the market.

But, optional extras aside, there’s still another issue that gets left behind in The Turn: does freedom alone even do what it’s meant to do? Are there important elements of this Social Success pattern that are being left to take care of themselves, and thus they wither on the vine as the system becomes more and more “pure”?

I ask this because the free market — at least in America, which is among its purest test cases — seems to be serving its purpose less and less well the “purer” it gets.

Take, for example, the financial crisis that started in 2008. It’s hard to recall how that happened and not see an example of the free market running amok — but rather than having me explain it, why don’t I take this opportunity to share this classic, laugh-out-loud funny yet robustly educational slideshow using cartoon stick figures to explain it on my behalf? 

The sheer, evil innovation here is free-market economics through and through.

There also used to be a kind of socio-economic contract between large businesses and workers, in which the workers devoted their careers to one entity, which showed something like loyalty to them by treating layoffs as an act of desperation, and providing health coverage and pension benefits. There’s nothing in free-market economics that dictates or even implies any of that, and it has all evaporated in recent decades, with companies shipping jobs abroad, eliminating pension plans, shifting to temporary workers, and engaging in leveraged buyouts whose payoffs dictate draconian cost-cutting, to name just a few. 

Why does modern-day capitalism seem so strained, so abstract, so “pure”? Because it used to be embedded in a society that knew not to take it too seriously: one where business leaders had outside values like community and patriotism to temper their acquisitive impulses; one where the notion of fiduciary responsibility, which nominally requires companies to consider only the interests of their shareholders, was tempered by the notion of companies as participants in their society as well; . But present-day business behavior seems to be shedding all of those distractions, and rushing headlong to execute the Free-Market pattern ever more purely. Result: stratospheric executive compensation, relentless short-term focus, obsession with stock price, consolidation into larger and larger entities, etc.

Where is The Turn in all this? It takes a plausible pattern associating an ecology of competition with congenial economic outcomes (especially compared to, say, feudal, aristocratic or authoritarian systems), infers that all congenial economic outcomes must arise from the free market, and then concludes that The More Free The Market The Better.

We’re all familiar with people and institutions that Take It Too Far: take a good idea and run with it so far that it breaks from the strain. I think The Turn is behind a great many of those.
