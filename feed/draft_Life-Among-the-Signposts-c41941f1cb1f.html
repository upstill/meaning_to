Life Among the Signposts

How vast is the range of the human experience. It’s hard to believe that one life can encompass experiences from the most crushing boredom…

Life Among the Signposts

One of the more sobering thoughts about being human is realizing how puny our mental faculties really are — how poorly equipped we are for comprehending the full scale of, well, pretty much anything outside our immediate experience. The universe, for example. Personally, I can’t get close at just the galaxy level: 100 billion stars just in our little corner of the universe — and something like a trillion more galaxies? Forget about it.

Same in the opposite direction. I can comprehend — barely — how small bacteria are in comparison to a human hair (roughly 100 times smaller, FYI), but an atom, 10,000 times smaller than that? Or a quark, millions and millions of times smaller still? The best we can do to appreciate such scales is to admit, sheepishly, how ill-equipped we are to appreciate such scales.

But forget the universe and just focus on the level of us humans. Can we even get our brains around the dynamic range of life on earth? Right now, there are people living in the darkest pits of despair and desperation, clinging to life by their fingernails, overwhelmed by natural disasters or just the everyday reality of their lives. People in prison by the millions, miserable at the hands of professional misery-makers. Children whose flesh is being shattered by wars they don’t even slightly comprehend. But there are still others for whom every moment is a radiant gift, who glow through life in a Valhalla of privilege and comfort.

We can name these situations just as we can reel off the billions and trillions in talking about the universe. But what would it take to really fit into our heads the full range of the reality of us humans? This grotesque poverty of our faculties is embarrassing, but also understandable: we evolved with brains whose sole purpose was to keep us alive long enough to reproduce, a very different charter than coping with anything like the full scope of reality. Upshot: we encode our experiences (in the moment) and our memories (in retrospect) with merciless frugality, eliding the non-essential and reducing everything to its essence. This is nowhere more evident than in the conceptualization embodied in language, wherein we: take a broad range of experiences; find their common essence, with special attention to what we care about; and associate that essence with a symbol — usually a word — that we can use to refer to any particular case. The word or symbol is a very useful — but limited — signpost pointing to a far richer experience. In general, the symbol is enough to get us through the day; recovering the richness of the source takes will and effort. Parsimony pays, bigly: in reduced cognitive effort, in saving attention for when it really matters, in defining symbols and abstractions that cover a vast range of reality.

This is one of the hallmarks of the human condition. It’s not just that we reduce stupefying realities to a vestige that will fit into our heads. We do that voluntarily as a matter of course, blithely unaware that we do it. But do we care about that? Does it really matter? Oh yes. Yes it does.

Signposts to God

There’s a great idea, attributed to Sufism, that “all religions are a signpost to God.” All the trappings of any given faith — assembly, ritual, practice, doctrine, iconography, hierarchy, culture, all of its earthly manifestations—are essentially just window dressing for The Real Thing. Behind and beyond that presentation lies what religion is really about: an experience of transcendence so powerful, so real, so true to those who have experienced it, that humanity can fairly be divided into those who have, and those who haven’t. Some people actually experience, in the most viscerally overwhelming way, the thread that connects them with the rest of humanity, with all life, with the cosmos. The difference between actually feeling that at a cellular level and holding those connections as an idealistic abstraction — words in a book — is like the difference between truly comprehending the scale of a galaxy and using the world “big”.

(Note: you may have an opinion about how “real” such experiences are—I know I do—but that’s beside the point: they’re real to the people who have them.)

Transcendence doesn’t necessarily play well with regular life, and so religions come laden with human hazards: reinterpreting the experience, bringing their own limitations and pathologies to it, trying to proselytize it, even just forgetting. But religion as expressed in the world can only be a signpost, a wan representation of…that other thing. Something vast. And it’s closely related to the data reduction of finding symbols for experience that will fit in our heads.

These kinds of data reduction are breathtaking, inevitable, and sad, but the real problem is that so many people never get past the signpost. They mistake the signpost for the thing it’s pointing to. They forget that church is not just about certain beliefs, or a community, or moral instruction; it’s supposed to be about experiencing the Divine, about transcending the mortal realm. They reduce the most profound experiences of human existence to their earthly representation, just as we reduce millions of starving children to the symbol “poverty”, or the prospective death of the planet to “climate change”.

Language can become a screen which stands between the thinker and reality. This is the reason why true creativity often starts where language ends.
 — Arthur Koestler

Life Among The Signposts

It makes perfect, practical sense to compress experience to fit into our heads. But having collected those signposts, why do we forget what they point to; forget, even, that the signpost represents anything else? We dismiss the wonder of the universe, both above and below. We satisfy ourselves that the symbols of language are all we need to know about our experience. We confuse the trappings of religion with its vast essence. 

Now here’s the point of all this:

We’re walking right by the opportunity to plumb the depths of our own, ordinary experience.

I live a pleasant ten-minute walk from my local hardware store. I can get there and back without my attention ever departing from what I’m going to buy and what I’ll do with it when I get home. But what would happen if I actually opened myself to the full experience of what’s around me on the way? The staggering variety of sights, sounds and feelings that journey offers: the endless variety of plants and homes; the texture of a leaf; the dense arrangement of leaves on a tree; the sometimes comical color schemes on display (What were these people thinking?); the flowers; the weeds; the undulating sidewalk uplifted by tree roots; the variegated humans passing by; the psychology of drivers as expressed in their driving; the extraordinary weathervanes on display; everything down to the colors and patinas of the cars in sight. I could be taking that walk several times a day for years and still be noticing things anew.

Or: I can go to the store, buy my screws, and go home.

Now consider human relationships, what we mean by symbols like “friends” and “family”, for example. Most of us know what such relationships are like during a crisis: when we’re brutalized by the death of a beloved, or a divorce, or a similar life-shaking event, we see each other in a very different light, painfully intimate in all our vulnerability and suffering. Is there some reason that it can’t always be like that? Why aren’t the humans always intimately connected? Conversely, why on earth do we count ourselves satisfied with the “connection” provided by social media? Or by a “community” of fellow churchgoers who are mainly about showing up and showing off?

But I don’t even need to get out of my chair to access rapture. Is the act of breathing just a bellows-like movement of air? Or is it a symphony of sensation from the tip of the nose to the depths of the belly, reminding us at every moment how intimately we are supported by our environment? Even that is just a sample of the vast range of bodily experience waiting for us, if we look at our bodies as more than just sporadically troublesome carrier vessels for our brains.

You see the difference, but what difference makes the difference? I submit that the bulk of it can be accounted for by a single variable: attention. Rather than treating everything in our path as a checklist item to be recognized and otherwise ignored, properly attending to what’s happening right now is the doorway to a limitless richness of experience.

What determines the quality of our lives, really? Our consumerist society certainly has its answer: More! More things, more experiences, more spending, more debt. More signposts that promise lasting satisfaction but turn out to be empty pointers leaving us grasping for the next thing. But have we truly experienced what we have already got? What if, instead, we traded breadth of experience for depth?

What if, having bought something, we set our minds on getting the absolute most value out of it — and only bought things that would reward such attention?

What if we decided to experience all of our relationships with the vulnerability and compassion we associate with falling in love?

What if we decided to marinate in gratitude, all day every day?

What if we decided to invest our attention, rather than our money, in living a richer life?

What, in other words, if we determined to recognize signposts for what they are, and to check out what they’re pointing to?

You might think that this is all a cynical exercise. It’s easy enough to ponder the poverty of our experience, to struggle to comprehend how vast the universe is, in all its facets, compared to our measly intellect, and realize that we can’t even comprehend that — and stop at “Too bad so sad.” But there’s another way to look at it. In truth, I’m trying to convey an opportunity. Here we are, stuck in our little meat packets, and there’s more out there than we can ever know! Isn’t that exciting? No matter where we look, we can gorge ourselves forever on richer and richer experience, to the very limits of our capacity. And all we have to do is: attend.

…but there’s a catch. I’ve established the sad inadequacy of our senses and our faculties in general, and our real need to reduce experience to signposts that we can deal with. Both things are manifestly, persistently true. Consequently, it’s critical to remember that “waking up” is an unnatural act. The option is and will remain invisible — unless and until we remember to pursue it. It just will remain effortful and relentlessly forgettable. It’s a permanent struggle and a matter of constant reminding (re-minding). 

I could go on, but why? Instead, I’ll sign off with a poem, from Wisława Szymborska, that other Polish Nobel-laureate poet. The title is Miracle Fair.

Commonplace miracle:
that so many commonplace miracles take place.

The usual miracle:
invisible dogs barking
in the dead of night.

One of many miracles:
a small and airy cloud
is able to upstage the massive moon.

Several miracles in one:
an alder is reflected in the water
and is reversed from left to right
and grows from crown to root
and never hits bottom
though the water isn’t deep.

A run-of-the-mill miracle:
winds mild to moderate
turning gusty in storms.

A miracle in the first place:
cows will be cows.

Next but not least:
just this cherry orchard
from just this cherry pit.

A miracle minus top hat and tails:
fluttering white doves.

A miracle (what else can you call it):
the sun rose today at three fourteen A.M.
and will set tonight at one past eight.

A miracle that’s lost on us:
the hand actually has fewer than six fingers
but still it’s got more than four.

A miracle, just take a look around:
the inescapable earth.

An extra miracle, extra and ordinary:
the unthinkable
can be thought.

People ask about the meaning of life. It’s a question that I’ve always spurned as meaningless (so to speak). But there’s an answer right here: life has an infinity of meanings, a literally infinite banquet, if we just go beneath the surface and look. With the right kind of eyes, we find all the meaning, all the richness, all the depth we’ll ever need, surrounding us, waiting for us to follow the signposts.

Speaking personally, that’s all the religion I need. I invite you to take this essay as a signpost. What is it pointing to?

Postscript: a few suggestions

There are two basic strategies for diving into meaning: 1) seek out high-impact experiences, or 2) open up to everyday experience by digging down and finding rapture in the everyday.

Going for high-impact experiences may sound like cheating (exactly what’s the signpost we’re getting past?), but opening our eyes does mean working upstream against past habits, and a powerful, arresting experience drags you there by force. That’s valuable if only as a demonstration of what it feels like to go beyond, so that you know what you’re aiming for, and recognize it when you get there.

So what are these “high-impact experiences”? I’ve written before (Why Everything Fades) about hedonic adaptation, by which any organism files the edges off and tamps its response down when re-experiencing the familiar. One key to overcoming that tendency is novelty. Travel does it, but so does anything that pushes at the boundaries of prior experience and/or your comfort zone: having a challenging conversation, developing a new skill, throwing yourself into a different social situation, even something as simple as checking out a new restaurant or taking a different route home.

But one kind of experience is especially fruitful: anything which induces awe, the sense of accessing something larger than oneself. The list of awesome experiences is long — for example, encounters with moral heroism, massive communal experiences, nature, wondrous music and art, encounters with mortality — and there’s no space here to do it justice, so I recommend Dacher Keltner’s book with the somewhat cheesy title Awe: The New Science of Everyday Wonder and How It Can Transform Your Life.

But how does one find rapture in the everyday, without overhauling our lives or becoming thrill seekers? There is a technology, practiced by millions, that’s been under development for thousands of years: mindfulness meditation. In its simplest essence, mindfulness is about foregrounding what’s happening right now by reducing how much we’re distracted by revisiting the past and rehearsing the future.

This is no mean feat, and it entails a consistent practice. But the difference between never practicing, and practicing even ten minutes a day, is not to be underestimated. I like Henry Shukman’s metaphor: if you have a container full of muddy water, but you habitually add a little clear water, eventually the whole container comes clear.

Speaking of Henry Shukman: there are countless approaches to mindfulness; without rejecting any of them, I recommend Shukman’s app The Way, which offers a training for accessing the full spectrum of experience, from simple mindfulness on up to full awakening. If that sounds boring, let me ask: have you ever experienced the simple act of breathing in all its detail?

(For a little inspiration on the mindfulness front, try Stop Ignoring All the Mundane Miracles in Your Life and/or read why A Single Small Map is Enough For a Lifetime. You can also find a little coaching on recovering attention here. Finally, consider looking closely.)

There is a way to bring meaning and depth to the things you’re already doing: find your way to flow. 

Beyond simple obliviousness, another block to fully accessing current experience is the distraction of emotions — not the emotions themselves, but the effort to supress them. We expend enormous energy shoving down and suppressing unpleasant feelings, which is both costly (it’s hard to numb one feeling without numbing them all) and fruitless: they’re still there, no matter how firmly they’re suppressed, hanging around like car hulks in your front yard. Bringing them to the surface and actually feeling them can recover that energy, increase the dynamic range of experience, and — this is the crazy part—work through them (the usual metaphor is metabolize them) so that they shrink to a manageable level or disappear altogether. Certainly, opening yourself to shattering trauma is a bad idea absent professional intervention, but below that level, many of us have feelings that we could deal with but just don’t want to.

For my money, the avatars of this idea are Tara Brach, with her book Radical Acceptance, and Michael Singer’s book The Untethered Soul. In both cases, integrating their lessons is the work of a lifetime, but they don’t require overhauling your life: every step along the way is its own reward.

Finally, let me just highlight something intriguing about this way of living fully: no conversion, no surrender to a guru, no woo, no ascetic discipline, no grand program or complicated theology, no imposed expectations or other setups for disappointment. It’s pretty much just us and our minds and remembering to pay attention, with modest amounts of acquired skill and practice — even 10 minutes a day. And every little bit helps: every deliberate pause to breathe and take stock; every choice to actually go through a feeling rather than run from it or clamp down on it; every time we just stop — for even a moment — ruminating about the past or rehearsing the future and take refuge in this moment; every bit of awe, even five minutes of going outside and staring at the sky. Really!

Footnote: obviously the suggestions above are just what I have personally found. How do you get past the signposts to The Thing Itself? And what are your favorite such experiences? Please, do use the comments to share your own.
