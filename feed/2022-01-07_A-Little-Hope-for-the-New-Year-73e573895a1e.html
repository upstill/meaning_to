A Little Hope for the New Year

Looking for the trigger of change.

A Little Hope for the New Year

Many of us are struggling to maintain hope for humanity. The reluctance to get serious about a warming planet. The fiercely divided response to COVID. The global groundswell of naked authoritarianism. The culture of contempt, oozing from cable news and social media, that poisons our politics and makes it seem like we’ll never again accomplish anything good, noble or even competent. To name just a few.

And yet, I have hope. Without making any predictions, there is a hopeful scenario available. And that gives me a little dollop of cheer, which is what we want at the year’s outset, right?

I start from two premises: first, that human beings are fundamentally decent; and second, that modern life — at least modern political life — is an appalling carnival of humanity at its worst.

At first, these two tenets seem contradictory: if people are decent, why are they acting this way? And even if both are true, why would anything change? That’s a lot to unpack. In fact, unpacking it is where my argument lies.

Simple decency has not vanished

If you only attend to the public square, it’s hard to muster much belief in the decency of human beings. We see tribes at war, selfishness if not outright criminality as the standard in economic behavior, and politicians rigorously trained in venality and short-sightedness.

But look up from your phone and you get a very different picture. In my personal life, I don’t know anyone like that, and I suspect you don’t either. The people in my life distinguish themselves with their humanity, concern, engagement and basic decency. And this isn’t just a sampling error: I have met rage monkeys online who turned out to be perfectly decent souls when engaged with one-on-one. A little fraught, perhaps, but everything we know about how mammals engage with each other teaches us that behavior in public, especially behavior in competing groups, is no indicator of the core, private character of the individual, as expressed in the flesh. People have evolved to relate one-on-one and within small groups, using not slogans and abstractions but evolving relationships mediated by a vast system of cues and responses, from facial expressions, to body language, to apparent levels of arousal, to verbal and non-verbal communication at all levels of intimacy. We respond to the people near us in a way that is nowhere in evidence at the societal level.

I don’t mean to imply that people are uniformly excellent to each other one-on-one or that venality has no private expression. But when I consider the difference between what I experience with the people around me, vs. strangers in the public square, it’s pretty stunning.

Changing the way we are in public isn’t a matter of changing who people are. It’s about aligning public behavior with who we are (by default) in private.

A dire present

I confess that I take it as a hopeful sign that people treat each other with such scorn and derision online and in the political arena. It is a hopeful sign that people are embracing, distributing and promoting the most blatant nonsense. It is a hopeful sign that the news has become an outrage factory pitched at those already primed for high dudgeon. It is a hopeful sign that the algorithms in news feeds drive people insane.

What makes these things hopeful? There’s an old saying that it’s always darkest just before the dawn. I thought that was completely ludicrous when I was a child, and it hasn’t improved much since, but it turns out to actually express something true and important.

Darkness is hopeful because that’s what drives change. Mediocrity is the enemy of improvement.

It’s only when matters devolve into the darkest darkness that the choice between muddling through with the status quo, and risking the uncertainties of change, tips toward the latter. In fact, and ironically, it is our very despair at the status quo that motivates deliberate change.

Are you despairing enough yet?

What would change look like?

If people are actually better than they seem in public, and if life as we know it is desperate enough to trigger a reaction, what might a reaction look like? Let’s take a quick trip down Fantasy Lane:

  • An understanding emerges that questioning everything is only the first step toward a free mind.
  • Cable news and social media become recognized as a toxic disease vector for alienation and contempt. Perhaps one network declares it their mission to be different, and they actually win viewership?
  • Some politician or politicians put a stake in the ground that their bailiwick is finding solutions, not rubbishing their opponents; that re-establishing effective governance is Job One. And they attract support from an exhausted, circumspect majority.
  • Posting disinformation online becomes Not Okay. Individuals who knowingly mislead others become treated as parasites feeding on the social fabric.
  • A recognition dawns that righteous wrath online doesn’t denote actual rightness, but is rather the self-declaration of a dopamine junkie. Word gets around that the feeling of certainty is a function of neurophysiology, not true belief.
  • Public figures like Alex Jones, peddling nonsense for profit, and Tucker Carlson, trading in outrage on the cheap, become social pariahs, alongside Big Pharma executives and people defrauding the elderly.
  • People start taking individual responsibility for the spread of toxic memes, pausing to think twice about forwarding that link that gave them a little dopamine hit of righteousness.
  • The practice of building and hardening information silos becomes identified as deeply unpatriotic, and honest engagement with the Other ascends as a prized value. We learn to appreciate good-faith information when we see it, and become averse to naked appeals to vitriol and cognitive bias. Shamelessness attracts shaming and shunning. Calling out bad actors —especially in one’s own camp — becomes an act of virtue.
  • Participating in a “discussion” that devolves into contempt and ad hominem attacks becomes seen as a failure, and online discussions become a competition for the moral high ground. The first person to bring up Hitler loses.
  • Attention becomes recognized as a private resource; respecting and managing this limited resource becomes a matter of personal hygiene and responsibility.
  • People develop a resistance to clickbait headlines. If we can learn to outwit Nigerian royalty scams we can learn to recognize appeals to impulse when we see them.

Didn’t that feel good? Can we just linger for a moment and savor the feeling from this round of Wouldn’t It Be Nice?

Now, back in reality, we can see how far we are from that world…or are we?

The story we make

Without a doubt, the reality above would be a momentous change, and it’s natural to associate momentous changes with hard work, moving heaven and earth to make something happen, winning elections and passing legislation and building new systems and breaking up Facebook and fixing human nature, and…and...and…

But this is not like that.

Every item on the laundry list above is a matter of headspace. Specifically, they’re about mores, ethics, standards of behavior. They don’t require changing minds, but changing attitudes. People easily become identified with their ideas; but the way they behave when they look at the world? As far as I know, no one has ever sworn allegiance to a poisonous information system. Seduced by it, for sure, but how much loyalty does it command? The question hardly even makes sense.

What drives such shifts of attitude? The best answer I know is: a good story.

Maybe a story about a desperate situation (the impending collapse of civility and democracy), with good guys (honest brokers) and bad guys (bad-faith demagogues).

Or, there’s the story of The Matrix: the idea that our reality is only a computer simulation, and while we think we’re walking around in the sunshine, that’s only an illusion: our brains are fed a comprehensive but false reality as our bodies are harvested for energy. The act in the movie of taking the red pill and learning the truth is already used as a metaphor by a wide swath of conspiracy theorists. Could we be just a meme away (“That’s what they want you to believe!”) from the wariness of information sources that’s called for?

Another meme might be aimed at exporting the standards of decorum that we use one-on-one and in small gatherings for personal and family issues, to political issues and discussions with strangers in the strange channels of the Internet and social media. It could be as simple as raising a persistent background question: Would I talk this way to my mother? Do I really want the world to see me as a fulminating blowhard?

And history has shown that there’s nothing like a common enemy to bring people together. Perhaps a story that tells the tale of humanity itself as a tribe with a shared interest in getting at the truth, where the enemy is the fog of tribal warfare, bad-faith argument and active deceit.

Why? Why not?

Anyone can think of countless reasons why this is all fantastical thinking at an embarrassing level of naiveté. But, at the same time, few things are as powerful in the world as a good story. The civil rights struggle began as a story of unfairness and oppression that was plain for all to see (on the evening news — which everybody watched!). It didn’t take over all at once; sufficient it was and is for a few people to take up the story and infect others with it. If it’s a good enough story, and minds are primed for it, the snowball rolls on until it’s hard to remember how things were ever so bad in the past.

All that has changed is minds, and only by taking a single story to heart.

How does that happen? How does a story take over? Since we are social creatures, minds change in large part when they perceive other minds changing. (Note that it doesn’t really matter whether they are in fact changing; only that others sense that change is afoot.) It is possible, but not predictable, to envision a wave of sensibleness that transforms the way we look at information and its bearers. The tragedy of our modern crisis is that it’s built on nothing more tangible than brain cells intercommunicating. The magic is, that’s also its solution.

All it takes is a Population 0 having a change of mind, and a further population primed to being infected with the idea that this is how it should be.

But no one, no matter how naive, should expect anything more than an incremental change. Even well-established standards that everyone agrees on get violated all the time. Men still beat their wives, thievery abounds, and people can still be appalling when no one is looking. Still, the fact that we do agree that these things are Not Okay improves the lay of the land: think how much worse off we would be without such principles. And that’s exactly where we stand now vis-a-vis the anarchy — and worse — of online behavior. Even a burgeoning ethic that misinformation is uncool would be a radical improvement in the back alley that is our Internet. Some people will always act out in public just as they do in person. But a little shame would go a long way.

To summarize:

  • People are better than we give them credit for, at least in private. We don’t need people to change qua people so much as we need private standards to go public.
  • The situation as it stands is primed for change just by how awful it is; our disgust at it represents a latent demand for a better information ecosystem.
  • Radical changes in standards can occur for the wispiest of reasons: it is not necessary for an individual’s attitude to actually change as it is for them to perceive that the attitude of others has changed.
  • Cultural shifts can happen like the end of a game of Janga, i.e., everything goes on as usual until one small difference makes all the difference.
  • Predicting such events in a chaotic world is a fool’s errand. Who knows which will be the last block, when the whole system comes tumbling down? But hope is not the same thing as prediction.

This is a good place to mention the End of History Illusion. When asked how they will change in the next ten years, people have a very hard time imagining being any different — whereas they have no trouble recognizing how much they’ve changed in the last ten years.

As with individual destinies, so with cultural norms. Slavery, child labor and spousal rape used to be part of The Way Of The World…until they weren’t. They haven’t vanished, but the tide has turned against them. Bigly.

One of my favorite lines from Lily Tomlin/Jane Wagner is “Reality is nothing but a collective hunch.” Some people love the idea that the world is a mental phenomenon and, as such, is malleable under mentation, i.e., we can get what we want if we just wish hard enough. Others like me consider that the most ludicrous hogwash if the goal is physical, material change. But what if the mental activity is the reality? What if the goal is just a change of mind? If the reality is cultural, then no matter how oppressive, no matter how pervasive, that reality can be transformed just by a shift in mental activity. The wonderful thing about our epistemic crisis is that it is pure mentation, stem to stern. Therefore, all it takes is a transformation in thinking.

How does such a change happen? It’s a capital-M Mystery, and it’s irreducibly unpredictable.

But we can have hope, can’t we?
