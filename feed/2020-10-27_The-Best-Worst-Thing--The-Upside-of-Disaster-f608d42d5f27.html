The Best Worst Thing: The Upside of Disaster

The very specific condition under which even the worst disaster can be a net positive. 2020, anyone?

The Best Worst Thing: The Upside of Disaster

Everyone knows Newton’s Third Law of Motion: “For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction.” Newton was referring to physical dynamics, but we apply the principle far more widely, especially in human affairs. His law pertains to people because we react to what happens to us. You see a looming vehicle, you get out of the way. You see an economic downturn coming, you sell off your investments. A plane crash hits the news, people opt for terrestrial transport. A Democratic politician decries mass shooting, gun sales skyrocket. The Japanese bomb Pearl Harbor, the US enters WWII in the Pacific.

Missing the Obvious

Such interactions (inter-actions) are so commonplace and unremarkable that they’re easy to forget— you know, water, fish? This year I have been amazed to hear pundits fretting because half of Americans say they wouldn’t take a COVID vaccine. I say “amazed” because why would you even think about something that won’t even be an issue for at least a year, probably two, i.e. until after 1) there is more information about it because, you know, it actually exists, 2) it is widely available, and 3) the less skeptical half of the population has been vaccinated. In the interim we can expect any number of intervening events: news reports about the approval process, colloquial reports from acquaintances who get it, more and more COVID, insurance coverage, employer interactions and other social proof. People will react to all of these and more, so their predictions now of how they will feel about vaccinations then aren’t worth the bits used to encode them.

I’m also surprised when some looming event triggers forebodings of disaster that completely discount — or ignore entirely — the mitigating effects of reaction. How many dire warnings have you heard about an impending road closure bringing on a traffic apocalypse? A freeway in traffic-choked LA closes for repairs. Does the entire region tip over into gridlock? It almost never plays out that way. Far from chaos descending, traffic actually eases — enormously. Why? Because drivers react to the looming disaster in all sorts of ways, from re-routing to staying home. The reason Y2K wasn’t a crashing disaster was that it was so vividly and anxiously expected to be a crashing disaster.

(It’s amusing to realize that the doomsayers drumming up fear and loathing actually trigger the mitigating reaction. In other words, they play an essential role in making themselves wrong!)

Opposite, but not Equal

What’s more interesting about human reactions, though, concerns the other part of Newton’s law: we often get opposite reactions, but we rarely see equal ones. You would expect civilization to react to nuclear-power incidents like Three-Mile Island, Chernobyl and Fukushima, and indeed it did. The development of nuclear power is basically moribund in the wake of deaths numbered in the dozens, leaving a coal-fired infrastructure that kills hundreds of times as many every year. By contrast, climate change is bringing on a radically degraded terrestrial environment, and our actions to head it off aren’t nearly up to scratch.

In my experience, the scale of the human reaction to an event is unrelated to the scale of the event itself (that is, its effects). Which would be amusing, except for one thing about this emergent system (event + reaction): the ultimate outcome, the net effect, of an event, is the effect of the event itself, offset by the effect of the reaction. If the countervailing reaction to a negative event is “smaller” than the event, the event will be, on the whole, negative. But if a negative event triggers a larger compensatory reaction, its overall effect will be a net positive. Negative events, no matter how large and seemingly catastrophic, can turn out to be good news in the long run, depending on the reaction they foment. The killing of George Floyd and other fatal encounters between police and Black people, horrific as they are, fit right into the landscape of violent crime. But in the context of race relations as a whole, they have triggered a reaction — an entirely appropriate reaction — of profound importance, bringing to account the history and the presence of racism in America.

Where we are now

Which brings me to two of the most wretched realities we’re living with right now: the breakdown of civility and shared reality on social media; and the degradation of civil life under Donald Trump.

The Internet was around for some time before Facebook and Twitter arrived on the scene, and its denizens already had an, um, casual relationship to reality. But just as Fox News has weaponized right-wing counter-bias, the attention economy of “free” social services has brought us to the point of an existential crisis in how we as a society are with each other. As I wrote in One Crisis to Rule Them All, the best indicator of just how broken our shared reality is, is the preposterous ideas that are now taken seriously, in public, by thousands if not millions of people. QAnon, Flat Earth, the “link” between vaccines and autism, etc., together with the widespread disdain (on both Right and Left) for “the mainstream media” (i.e., professional journalism with standards of integrity and verification), are the bright red flashing emergency lights of an information ecosystem in collapse…

…so much so that the issue is getting unprecedented attention both inside and outside the companies responsible: within government, talk of breaking them up; within the companies themselves, actually banning threads of blatantly untrue and harmful content. To be clear, this is a very hard problem to tackle — and it strikes at the heart of a business model that has made many a billionaire. But that just tells you how dire the situation must be if it’s being addressed this seriously. Is it that hard to imagine a time in the not-too-distant future when people will shake their heads in wonderment and disdain at the bumpkins we must have been to allow such nonsense to spread so freely? Of course, it may not play out that way, but can you imagine it playing out that way if the Internet had loped along with its drip-feed diet of silly links and cat videos?

The Fruit of Trumpism?

At this point we are all embedded in the chaos that is Donald Trump. But there may come a time when the darkness lifts and we exit our caves, blinking, into a post-Trump day. What could that be like?

  • By attacking them at every turn, hasn’t Trump sharpened our appreciation (to put it mildly) for democratic norms, from financial transparency to the separation of powers? For the normal operation of government, in which professionals manage things like national security and criminal justice free of storms from above?
  • With his congenital lying, hasn’t he given us a new respect for truth and those who provide it, from civil servants to flawed but earnest media outlets?
  • Hasn’t his caustic Twitterocracy left a craving for leadership that makes us feel more included, united, and…decent?

But it’s not just Donald Trump. In recent years, Republicans have plumbed the depths of fealty before him and embraced a depraved, power-first, principal-be-damned stance toward governance. Heralded by Mitch McConnell’s express intent to deny Barack Obama any positive achievements, it descended this year to a head-snapping about-face over election-year Supreme Court nominations, and their program of undermining the 2020 election by word and deed. After making the Republican Party his thrall, Trump will leave it a disgraced husk that needs to earn its way back from the wilderness, perhaps adopting a tone of decency and reasonableness, a refreshed spirit of compromise, a morning-after sense of shame. Maybe not (I’m not holding my breath, though see this year’s upsurge in young-voter participation, or listen to these suburban women describe the shift in their attitudes), but it’s far more likely than it would have been after more years of business as usual.

In contrast, where would we have been after four years of a Hillary Clinton presidency? Four more years of Republican intransigence lightly papered over by conservative rhetoric. Four more years of scrabbling to eke out incremental improvements in health care or climate change policy. Racial tension and hostility to immigrants simmering along just under the surface. Another wan swing of the pendulum between middle-of-the-roaders in the two parties. Where’s the change in that?

Thanks to Donald Trump, the ideal of common governance; our fellow feeling as Americans; any sense of a shared destiny that we need to achieve together; any ability to work within a shared reality; even the idea of not giving in to naked impulse, selfishness and ill will; all that has its back against the wall. Can we, will we, react with a desperate lunge for the sweet refuge of a civil environment built on respect, a recovery of civic decency? Joe Biden seems to have grasped that thirst. If it is answered, Donald Trump will have done more good, in the most ironic possible way, than any boilerplate Democrat or Republican ever could have.

I am not making any predictions, much as I would love to see it play out this way. My point is that great change, real impetus in the world, doesn’t come spontaneously. It doesn’t always come just because it’s needed, either. It doesn’t happen when we just kind of want it, or think “I guess that would be a good idea”, but when we’re desperate, when there doesn’t seem to be any other way than to stand up and say “Enough!”. The pickle we’re in is horrific, but it also bears uniquely promising seeds of change precisely for being so dire. If the reaction to this current world is stronger than the hideousness of our situation, we really could redeem this darkness. And, ironic as it is, we will have Donald Trump to thank.
