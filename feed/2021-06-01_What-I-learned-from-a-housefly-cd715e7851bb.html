What I learned from a housefly

Coming to appreciate chaos and diversity.

What I learned from a housefly

One sunny afternoon, I was in my bedroom and I noticed a fly trying to get out through a window. The window itself was a large fixed pane, with open panels on each side, but the fly was trying to fly out through the unyielding middle.

This was not an intelligent critter, but it did have room in its head for a really smart strategy. You might think that such a simple organism might just pick a spot to fly through and keep banging into it there, over and over. But no. Instead, it would try one place, then divert randomly left or right and try again…and again…and again. Such a random walk (or fly) doesn’t seem much shrewder than just banging away in the same place (indeed, it doesn’t take much more brain power), except for one important difference: trying the same thing over and over without variation is doomed to failure, but introduce a random variation and you’re actually doomed to succeed — as the fly showed when it eventually wandered into one of the open panels and flew away. It didn’t have to know about the openings. It didn’t need some grand plan for reconnoitering the situation. It just used almost the simplest possible strategy.

This fly didn’t exactly teach me about stochastic perturbation (!), but it did leave me with a superb metaphor for navigating through life. We all have a fairly clear idea, reinforced by self-help tomes beyond numbering, about how to pursue a goal: 1) decide where you want to go, 2) suss out a sensible route from here to there, 3) drive forward along that path until you get there. What separates the adults from the children, the successful from the strivers, is that word “drive”: the determination to let nothing stand in the way, overcoming all obstacles. You may have to build habits, overcome self-doubt, learn to self-promote, sacrifice other priorities, consult the stars, buy nutritional supplements, whatever. But it all comes down to: push, push, push.

The mythos of the self-made man is just that simple: if you only try hard enough (push through your own laziness, overcome all resistance, make it happen no matter what), you will eventually succeed. But it’s not just this masculine ethic of overpowering the world, and it’s not just the American pioneer/Horatio Alger vision of conquering the land with your own blood, sweat, toil, tears and gumption: the basic idea behind The Secret is that you can bend the universe to your will by using only…your will. But no matter what form this principle takes, your reality and your achievements are just a test of character: succeed, and the credit is yours alone; if you fail in tailoring the world to your liking, it’s your fault.

It’s an alluring strategy. It makes sense, lends clarity, appeals to our Puritan work ethic, gives people a way to separate themselves from the competition, and generally seems to work. It’s an all-purpose recipe for success, insofar as it applies to anything you might want, from riches and fame, to community, to love. But like so many alluring ideas, it matters for what it leaves out as much as what it includes. As the fly will tell you, what it leaves out is randomness. Chance. Serendipity. Luck.

Grace.

Luck and Success

Looking back, I’m in awe of the role that chance has played in my life. I didn’t choose to go into computing because I knew that tech was becoming a thing; I set out to do computers because one fine day I came home from school to find in my mailbox a magazine with an article about long-haired freaks spending their working days developing and playing videogames. (This was 1972.) I didn’t set out to do computer graphics at a time when a dozen people in the world, at most, were making a living at it; I happened to be walking across campus with a friend when he casually mentioned an upcoming course being given by people from George Lucas’s company; if I hadn’t heard about it then, I’d have missed out. (This was 1979.) I didn’t set out to meet my wife in New York City when I was first in university; I just happened to see a notice for an off-campus project to spend a term living and working in NYC, which is where I met her.

In all those cases, two things happened: 1) something unexpected occurred, and then 2) I changed course to accommodate it. I could have kept plowing toward my initial EE degree. I could have decided to finish my first year of school before tearing off to New York. I could have carried on with my MS for a generic career in tech, rather than ultimately joining Pixar on its first day. And that’s what a relentless focus on goals and control leaves out.

Conversely, my experience is also dotted with incidents of risky behavior, where I got away clean from a bad decision that could have wrecked my life, and the difference was 100% luck. Surely I’m not the only one.

It’s worth noting that Steve Jobs was one of the most outspoken advocates for “following your dreams” no matter what — yet, as Cal Newport has said,

If a young Steve Jobs had taken his own advice and decided to only pursue work he loved, we would probably find him today as one of the Los Altos Zen Center’s most popular teachers.

Jobs didn’t follow his dreams. He followed the opportunities that fortune brought his way. His drive was peerless, but he had the good sense to know when to loosen his control.

You may be thinking that this interplay between drive and luck is obvious, but if you ask successful people what they attribute their success to, more often than not you’ll get an earful about how hard they worked and how determined they were; you won’t hear much about luck and happenstance — and this skewed perception has all sorts of unhappy consequences, from an unwarranted sense of entitlement to diminished empathy to, that’s right, conservative politics, which view any action to redistribute wealth from the more fortunate to the less so as theft from the deserving to the losers. As Robert Frank points out in his book Success and Luck[1], high achievers will argue that they worked hard for their achievements, and that’s often true. But other, equally talented, hardworking people did not succeed. What separates the two? Did they earn the genes for the aptitudes that got them there, the place in the world they were raised? Was their history really devoid of happy accident, of opportunities that came from nowhere, of mentors and other supportive souls who came along at the right time?

Luck Looking Forward

If the role of good fortune and happenstance is easy to miss in retrospect, it’s even harder to see it looking forward, if all you’ve got in mind is drive, purpose and overcoming obstacles. Fortune doesn’t always take the form of opportunities popping up in the road, and grace doesn’t always arrive as a chauffeur from the future. Sometimes they show up disguised as obstacles and interruptions. And sometimes, you can be surprised by what happens if you just loosen the grip that control has on your thinking.

At one point in my life, my wife and I hosted weekly Movie Nights: a potluck dinner party with a dozen or so guests, followed by a movie in our good-sized movie room — the wrinkle being that I picked the movie. (I didn’t even tell anyone what was coming up until it was all cued up and ready to go.) I only promised to show them something they would be glad they saw, even if they wouldn’t have picked it themselves. I like to think that they gave themselves the gift of letting go, and got the reward of serendipitous discovery. Maybe it was the food or the company that kept them coming back, but they did keep coming back — for more than a decade. Remarkably, of all the people that were invited to Movie Night over the years, only one steadfastly declined, refused to relinquish control. He wanted his cinematic diet to consist only of what he thought he would like. Isn’t that sad?

That’s a superficial example, but there’s one phenomenon, far, far out in the wilderness, that throws a stark light on any assumption that more autonomy and control is always, always better than less: arranged marriages, the practice of life partners being chosen for the betrothed by parents and extended family. In my culture, arranged marriages seem like a barbaric artifact of an unenlightened past. It must be a disaster to give up that much control, right? But in point of actual fact, arranged marriages generally work out quite well [2].

The institution of arranged marriage is fraught with obvious moral hazards. But it’s also a case study in how much we’re capable of accommodating, if we can loosen the grip of control-above-all on our thinking. Even a situation as out of control as an arranged marriage can have a happy ending. It may take a strong culture to make that happen, and a commitment to making it work, but someone who has the issue settled for them just might be better off than those who have to deal with the nightmare of dating and romantic uncertainty. It turns out that control and ultimate freedom of choice aren’t always the Platonic virtues we take them for.

Off-track is where the wins are

There are even goals where interruptions and digressions are not just a good idea, but a necessity. One of those is thinking — that is, navigating the landscape of ideas and trying to make sense of the world. If your goal is to refine your current thinking, to improve your internal map of the world outside, where are those improvements going to come from, if not from outside your current thinking? To put it starkly, every new thing you might be thinking will seem absolutely irrational — until it’s not. As Paul Graham points out in his essay How to Think for Yourself, new ideas are literally those which a population isn’t already thinking; the only way to get to them is to confront the (currently, seemingly) irrational. (He elaborates this point more explicitly in New Ideas.)

As with individual minds, so with cultures and civilizations. Is the way to build a civilization to figure out the best way to be, and then be that, to the exclusion of all else? I doubt that stagnant sameness should be our highest aspiration as a society no matter how well it’s honed and optimized, even if history weren’t roiling about us — but it is. Are culture and civilization even meant to be fixed and finalized, sooner or later? It might be that the peak of civilization is like the top of a mountain where no more climbing is necessary and matters may rest there forevermore. But isn’t it equally likely that progress is less a forward thrust than it is a ferment, a process of experimentation and accommodation? And that the mark of a robust civilization is its capacity to flex and divert in the face of the new?

If that’s true, then you’re not allowed to reject ideas just because they’re off the reservation as currently defined. We don’t have to either accept or believe them; we just have to recognize that the good ideas of the future are likely to be found among the bad ideas of today, and it’s the very process of working through bad ideas that keeps us on our toes, both as individuals and as whole societies.

Not only can we not know which of today’s bad ideas will be the good ideas of tomorrow, but calling bad ideas out into the open — as opposed to censoring or otherwise suppressing them — can teach us lessons that are seemingly unrelated. This is why the appalling parade of delusional nonsense in today’s public sphere (QAnon, Flat Earth, election fraud, etc.), as well as the off-the-rails history of the Trump presidency, just might be useful, i.e. if they shock and alarm the culture into growing more sophisticated about dealing with information bubbles, tribalism, confirmation bias and malignant narcissism in positions of power. If it does, that will be precisely because the current situation is so heinous.

Diversity reconsidered

To me, cultural ferment by actively engaging with the “abnormal” (as currently understood) is the single greatest argument for cultural diversity. In a way, our present-day culture wars come down to a battle between the model that says that “our” society is as good as it’s going to get, and those who believe in their heart of hearts that difference and diversity are valuable just because they’re different, because they plant seeds of change that make the society more robust — and this is true even if they’re wrong! The only way to sort out the valuable ideas from the inimical is to throw them against the wall and see which stick. The best case scenario is that diversity provides the impetus for useful change; at worst, it makes us appreciate how good we’ve got it. In any event, sorting through such issues at the edge of our collective comfort zone is good exercise for a culture.

But never mind change over time. Even in the present, no group can do without rich internal variation. Most of us are intuitively leery of groupthink and the “madness of crowds”, but James Surowiecki, in his book The Wisdom of Crowds, put his finger on what distinguishes a smart crowd (which, for example, can guess the weight of a bull at a county fair within a few pounds) from a dumb one (which thought it was a great idea to storm the US Capitol building). When the members of a decision-making body are uncorrelated with one another in their thoughts (meaning, essentially, that diversity of thought is actively promoted), and they provide their input outside a context where groupthink can prevail, then their chances of success are vastly improved. Do I even need to paint a picture of echo chambers as cesspools of correlation?

The Joy of Randomness

Of course, my fly had none of these lofty ideas in whatever it had for a mind. It reached its goal of getting away just by incorporating randomness into its strategy, even while maintaining its unrelenting purpose. And this is the purest, scariest form of relaxing control. Can we actually roll the dice with our lives, throw ourselves entirely on the mercy of Fate?

That’s a scary prospect — until we start to notice processes where success depends on a certain amount of randomness. Consider evolution by natural selection. Selection is meaningless without a diverse set of variations to be selected among. The source of the variation driving evolution on Earth is a matter of hot debate, but one aspect of it is not: nobody is in charge of deciding how to steer the process or which variations to try and which not. The fact that the system is literally out of control is its beauty and its strength.

The Leap of Faith

The development of life on Earth is all well and good, but don’t we want matters in our individual lives to feel a little more regular? You would think so, but the fact is that our lives already abound with situations where you have to commit to a path before you know what the outcome will be — and there’s no going back once you start. The outcome may not be random, but it is deeply uncertain; such situations come with an irreducible element of risk, which is to say they are fundamentally, definitionally out of control.

Parenthood is one example. Every first-time parent learns one thing right off the bat: they had no idea what they were getting themselves in for. Many parents thrive under the circumstances and others are crushed by it, but they all took the leap without having a clue how it would turn out — because how could they know?

I have done a little single-track off-road mountain biking, and one lesson comes rushing in straightaway: timidity is not your friend. Slowing down enough to pick your careful way over the next tree root or to ensure that you can make that hairpin curve is a challenge to your balance and a disaster for your ability to control the bike. The only thing for it is to fling yourself forward, giving up the idea of being safely in control and having faith in your skills to manage what comes up. And what’s exhilarating is: it works!

Human relationships are often like that. How many times have you been in a situation where there was something to say to another person that could either take the relationship to the next level or send it off the rails? There was no way to tell in advance how it was going to go, and you couldn’t un-say that thing once you said it, nor could they un-hear it. Confronted with that choice, you either stayed silent and let matters stagnate, or took the leap, counting on the ability of you and the relationship to get through the crisis even without knowing the outcome.

I like to call the decision to move forward with no assurance of success the leap of faith. It reminds me of jumping off a cliff into a fog bank. There may be a safe landing on the other side, but so might broken bones or a funeral. Either way, there’s no getting around the need to take the leap — if you want to live a full life. Such situations generally include the option of standing pat and doing nothing, in the name of keeping certainty and control. And how sad is that?

Seize the Distraction; Embrace the Obstacle

In pursuing goals with single-minded determination, when interruptions, distractions and obstacles present themselves, the automatic reaction is to see them as threats. But that reflex is at least questionable, because opportunities sometimes arrive disguised as obstacles, and recognizing them for what they are only takes a little thought. Blessed are the times that obstacles arise spontaneously, for that is when we get our blinders knocked off, and we have an implicit choice whether to get annoyed (because our purpose was violated), or to take it as an opportunity for exploration.

Brian Eno has described his creative process in a way that captures this interplay beautifully. He’ll set out on a project with a goal in mind, and work toward that goal until he encounters a problem. Then he sets out in a new direction suggested by the obstacle — until another obstacle presents itself. In this way he takes a path through the space he’s working in that weaves together intention and serendipity. Eno even developed (with Peter Schmidt) a set of dictums, dubbed Oblique Strategies, for breaking the creative blocks that occur when drive meets blockage, employing such seemingly Dada-esque instructions as “Ask your body” or “Gardening not Architecture”: anything to throw a spanner into the works of single-minded pursuit. And the finished work isn’t some construction that sprang whole from the forehead of the creator. It is informed and inspired in large part by the interruptions, and the process of working with them.

Randomness and Intentionality: a Dialectic

There are also ways to dip your toe safely and playfully in the waters of chance without really leaving that safe shore, and that can really be a refreshing exercise. Think about choosing music to listen to. Do you always go back to the music of your youth, or do you look for newer tunes that might be just as compelling. And how exactly does one do that?

Unsolicited plug: Spotify presents as the answer to this prayer, with its options for helping a person step knowingly outside the familiar. Starting with a familiar, beloved song or artist, you can ask for “more like this” and get just enough difference to be interesting. Once you establish your own listening patterns, the service produces daily and weekly Discover playlists, and a Release Radar playlist for new material by people you like. Even better, if you stumble on a new genre of music you like, as typified by a particular artist, you can launch a “… Radio” that presents you with a panorama of adjacent music. The exhilaration of finding something new that slots right into your brain is difficult to convey.

Parties are surprisingly similar, both in the strategy and the opportunity they present. One can spend a whole evening bantering and catching up with people you know — but you can also make it your purpose to engage with strangers. They won’t be that strange (after all, they are also connected to the party-giver), and almost everybody has something interesting to say. In fact, one can spend the entirety of a party on a mission to find the most interesting person in the room.

A Life Less Ordinary

The religious have the comfort of passing off the responsibility for their fate to a higher power. One of the disadvantages of secular life is that, by default, it dumps all responsibility for the way things turn out on the individual. That would be an enormous burden to carry even if we could manipulate all of the levers of reality to our liking, driving ourselves to just the destination we had in mind. But we can’t. The universe is random, dodgy and obstreperous in ways that are by turns infuriating and miraculous. There’s good news, though: coming to terms with the fact that it’s not all up to you is strangely liberating. Realizing that you’re embedded in systems beyond your control — and that you can learn to enjoy their company — means that it’s not all about you. It’s a powerful way of living lightly in the world.

Back at the beginning, I used the word “grace”. In Christian theology, it refers to expressions of God — basically blessings — in everyday life. In the absence of a divine entity, I think of grace as anything you can be grateful for that‘s not your direct responsibility. In the drive to achieve and keep focussed on goals, grace takes any number of forms that are easy to miss. As far as I’m concerned, staying alert to the opportunities that have so many different disguises, and dropping back from the chimera of full control and responsibility to invite serendipity in, is close to the essence of a graceful (grace-full) life.

This is what that fly crystallized for me: that a relentless forward path can be doomed to failure; how important a little arbitrary influence can be; and how education can come from the most surprising places — if you’re paying attention.
