Takeaways from 50 year of cooking

If you like to skim the cream from the experience of others, let me offer a few key gleanings from a life in the kitchen. Why should you care what I think? I’m not a professional cook, nor even a particularly distinguished amateur, but there’s one master lesson I’ve learned over time: Good cooking doesn’t have to mean tricky or difficult cooking. There are millions of recipes out there, and some of the simplest are some of the best. At the same time, there are a few cooking hacks that aren’t difficult at all but make a quantum difference in the outcome. It’s just a matter of paying attention. Skill and tricksiness are totally optional barriers to feeding yourself well.

  • Cooking for people and offering it to them is glue for bonding. Even (especially?) people who have no use for emotions and relationships expressed in any kind of overt fashion are touched by the act of sitting and eating together, and the offering of your makings. It’s been the foundation of my community (one that has no other obvious binding factors), and our kids have gone on to behave likewise just because the results were so obvious to them. So the meta-tip here is “Make a life for yourself by feeding people.”
  • It is a sin to waste meat carcasses. Instead of dumping them into the trash, dump them into a pot, cover generously with water, add a little salt (possibly some carrot, unpeeled garlic, bay leaf, etc. as well) and simmer for an hour. Then make soup! Almost any soup you make with a half-decent stock — no matter how simple — will blow the doors off almost any other soup that uses store-bought “broth”.
  • Keep an herb patch. Herbs are survivors: their hardiness is related to the volatile oils that provide the flavor. Many of them, like rosemary, parsley, mint, sage and thyme just keep going and going. There’s nothing more pleasurable than strolling outside and plucking a few leaves for your supper — unless it’s the pleasure of not paying multiple dollars for a small helping from the store. Even in pots, even just a parsley plant…
  • Get whole spices and grind them. I know, this sounds too twee for words, but again: volatile oils. They disappear fast from ground product. Using an old whirly coffee grinder takes seconds, and the smell that comes out when you open it…!
  • No recipe ever suffers from a third less sugar or twice as much garlic or vegetables.
  • Why, oh why, does anyone buy sugar syrup? A cup of water, a cup and a half of sugar, three minutes in the microwave, and a quick stir. Done.
  • …or salad dressing? Some oil, a little vinegar, salt and pepper and a quick whisk is all it takes. Elaborations (garlic, mustard, yogurt, chopped herbs, etc.) optional — and fun.
  • Do not disrespect the frozen pea, or corn, or canned tomato. These things are packaged near where they’re grown, so even when they’re seasonal I’ve rarely met a fresh pea to beat its frozen cousin.
  • Water is amazing…for thawing frozen foods. Its thermal mass gets heat into the frozen in a fraction of the time.
  • Measuring by volume and by weight are interchangeable, a lot of the time. The reason is that many foods are approximately as dense as water, and — what do you know? — 16 fl. oz. of water weighs…1 pound!
  • Sungold tomatoes rule. If you only grow one vegetable, let it be Sungold cherry tomatoes. Every year my wife plants tomato varieties by the dozens, and every year the most exciting moment is when the Sungolds start coming in.
  • Don’t be afraid of salting meat. 
  • The Maillard reaction is where the flavor is. From slowly browning onions to toasting nuts and roasting vegetables, to making gravy from the scratchy drippings in a roast pan, the magic that happens when surfaces are gently browned is the shortest, laziest path to richness and flavor across the board.
