Taking Refuge in the Present

A not-so-obvious truth about mindfulness

Taking Refuge in the Present

“Live in the Present!”

“All you have is Now.”

“Today is a gift. That’s why they call it The Present.”

“No matter where you go, there you are.”

I wonder if everyone is as weary and leery of these dictums (dictae?) as I am, their meaning dulled from sheer repetition. Mindfulness — living in the present moment — is a paradigmatic mantra of new-age thinking, the premiere virtue of a certain kind of secularism, and we all pay it some kind of credence. It makes sense, doesn’t it? To be where you are rather than be distracted by revisiting memories or rehearsing future disasters in your mind? Give me more of that, please!

But it also has something shared by many other virtues: an aspirational, ideal quality, a wouldn’t-it-be-nice right up there with “Live every day as if it’s your last”: the domain of an elect, setting themselves apart with character beyond the grasp of us poor distracted souls. Surely it must be a special kind of person who can stick with where they are, when they are, rather than be dragged hither and yon by turbulence of mind.

Finally, mindfulness seems a little like spinach: good for you in some indistinct, take-your-vitamins, get-enough-sleep kind of way that lumps it in with all those things we could be doing for ourselves, and would be doing if we knew what’s good for us. So what else is new?

I’ve been regularly meditating on a very low-key basis (ten minutes a day, using the Waking Up meditation app) for a couple of years now, and I’m still just a floundering amateur. But I have put my finger on something about it:

Mindfulness — actually being absorbed by what is happening now — is not an act of discipline and denial — a virtue, in other words —for humans to aspire to. It is actually a comfort, an indulgence — a refuge.

Let me explain — with full caveats not to mistake my voice as authoritative.

What Is Mindfulness?

There are many ways to approach meditation/mindfulness, but the common essence is this: observing the mind at work and developing a full awareness of what it’s up to as it goes about its business. In broad strokes, there are two facets to that: seeing cogitation for what it is; and finding out what conscious experience is like when cogitation and rumination subside.

The first facet is finding a way to look at thoughts from the outside, as opposed to being swept away in identification with them — to view them as something you do, as opposed to letting them define who you are. With a glimpse of this stance, you are shocked to realize how few thoughts are really necessary, how goddamn long they go on, how repetitive they are, so that the bulk of the time and effort you spend thinking is a wholly gratuitous waste.

By default, present experience is dominated (to put it mildly) by the past and the future: loops endlessly reviewing what has happened, or rehearsing/worrying about what the future might hold, or — my specialty — critically evaluating every move, from driving to crossing the room to folding toilet paper. Certainly, you can’t do without those kinds of thoughts altogether — just try to live without learning from the past or planning for the future — but if you learn one thing in meditation, it’s how useless the overwhelming majority of those thoughts are, from even this pragmatic perspective. And I do mean overwhelming, because they literally overwhelm what’s going on now, usurping all but a tiny fraction of conscious attention that could be spent attending to the moment, whether it’s pleasant or challenging.

What Practice Is For

There’s a reason meditation is called “practice”: unwinding these rumination loops, getting to where they fall away of their own weight, is devilishly tricky. Monkey mind is always clamoring to take over: just try to spend 30 seconds — or even 10 — without a thought popping up. But what happens when present experience is no longer all about the past or the future? You gain access to a whole landscape, facets of present experience that you’ve only known in theory, or in the grossest of detail: the sensations of your body; the sights, sounds and smells of the world outside; an intimate, responsive connection to the people nearby; the wispy breezes of emotion that float into view, then drift away; ideas and inspirations that are too ephemeral to stand up to the gales of thought that are normally blowing through your mind; showing up for challenges and facing them with your full self; being absorbed and fully committed to whatever you happen to be doing (in flow); and, finally, just an abiding sense of being, in which simply existing and paying attention is such a rich experience that it becomes all you need.

This is what I mean by taking refuge in the present. Achieving an attentive state of mind erects an almost literal barrier that keeps the past and the future where they belong — and gives you the space to be where you belong: right here right now. This is what the title is about: the present as refuge from all those ghosts of the past and speculations about the future.

It’s paradoxical to think of real mindfulness as a profound pleasure — the sensation of pleasure being just another thought — but the gratification is real. The relief is real. It’s like putting down a bag of bricks you’ve been carrying for as long as you’ve been sentient.

A Road Worth Traveling

Again, nobody will mistake me for any kind of adept. Anyone who knows me will attest that what I’m describing is 90% aspiration. So I’m not here to proselytize about some brave new world I’ve found, only to submit that I can see what lies down the road, and have tasted it, however sporadically. It’s not what I thought (as it were).

When it comes to a life well-lived, quality of attention is the coin of the realm — literally the stuff of life. Everything in the past has passed, and the future is imaginary. Why would you spend any more attention on them than you need to? What else is there, really, but this moment? Why wouldn’t you milk it for all it’s worth? How much are you giving up otherwise?

Carpe diem, indeed.
